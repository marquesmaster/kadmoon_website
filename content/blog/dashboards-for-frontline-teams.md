Walk through most organizations' reporting and you will notice something: nearly all of it is built for leadership. Executive dashboards, management packs, summaries for the people running the business. The people actually doing the work, the frontline, are left with little they can use, because the reporting answers leadership's questions, not theirs. This is a large missed opportunity, because the frontline is where the work happens and where a lot of the room to improve it lies. Here is why most reporting fails the frontline, and how to build dashboards for the people doing the work.

## Reporting is built for the top

Start by noticing the pattern: most reporting is built for **leadership.** It summarizes the business for the people managing it, answers strategic and management questions, and is designed for the view from the top. This is natural, leadership commissions the reporting and leadership's questions are the ones asked, but it means the reporting serves the people above the work, not the people doing it.

The frontline, the people on the floor, in the field, at the point of service, are left with reporting that is accurate, comprehensive, and useless to them. A summary of the whole business does not tell a frontline worker what they should do in their specific role right now. The leadership dashboard answers leadership's questions, and the frontline's questions, which are different, go unanswered. So the people actually doing the work are left without the information that would help them do it better.

This is a missed opportunity, and a large one, because the frontline is where the work actually gets done and where much of the room to improve it exists. Reporting built only for the top informs the people managing the work while leaving the people doing it in the dark, which gets the leverage backwards: the biggest practical gains are often at the frontline, and that is exactly where the reporting does not reach.

## A frontline dashboard is a different thing

A frontline dashboard is not a smaller version of a leadership dashboard; it is built around **different decisions.** An executive dashboard summarizes the business to inform strategic decisions, read occasionally, broad in scope. A frontline dashboard shows a **specific person what to do now** in their role, tied to the immediate operational decisions they make, often checked frequently, narrow and specific in scope.

The difference is in who it serves and what decisions it supports. Leadership makes broad decisions about the business and needs a summary; the frontline makes concrete, immediate decisions about the work and needs to know what to do right now. These are genuinely different briefs, and they produce genuinely different dashboards. Designing a frontline dashboard means starting from the frontline worker's **actual decisions**, what do they decide, how often, with what information, which are concrete and immediate, rather than from the summary view leadership wants.

This is the key shift: to build for the frontline, you design around the frontline's decisions, not leadership's. The same principle that makes any report good, start from the decision, applied to a different person's decisions produces a different, and for them far more useful, report.

## Immediate and specific

What makes a frontline dashboard work is **immediacy and specificity.** It shows the person what they should do now, in their specific role, rather than a broad summary of anything. It focuses on the few things that drive the decisions that person actually makes, presented so they can act on them at a glance, and it is current, because frontline decisions are made in the moment and stale information cannot guide them.

A good frontline dashboard answers one question well: **what should I do right now, given where things stand?** That is a sharper, more immediate brief than a leadership dashboard, which answers "how is the business doing?" The frontline question is concrete and tied to action, and meeting it means designing tightly around the specific work and decisions of the role, showing exactly what the person needs to act and nothing else. A frontline worker glancing at their dashboard should see, immediately and specifically, what to do, which is a different and more demanding kind of focus than a summary requires.

This is why a frontline dashboard cannot just be the executive dashboard shown to more people. The executive view answers the wrong question for the frontline, in the wrong terms, at the wrong level of specificity. The frontline needs its own view, built for its own immediate, specific decisions, and building that well is a distinct piece of design.

## Where operational improvement happens

The reason building for the frontline matters so much is that **a lot of operational improvement happens at the frontline.** The work gets done there, so the quality of the decisions made there directly determines how well the organization operates. Helping frontline workers make better decisions, through reporting tied to their real decisions, improves the work itself, in a way leadership reporting alone cannot.

This is the leverage that reporting built only for the top misses. Leadership reporting improves the decisions of the people managing; frontline reporting improves the work itself, at the point where it happens. For an organization serious about operational performance, equipping the people doing the work with the information they need is often where the biggest practical gains are, because it improves the many daily decisions that add up to how the organization actually runs. A better-informed leadership makes better strategic calls; a better-informed frontline does better work, and there is a lot of frontline.

So building dashboards for the frontline is not a lesser form of reporting; it is reporting aimed where much of the operational value is created. It is harder in some ways, because it demands understanding the specific, immediate decisions of each frontline role rather than the general view leadership wants, but it reaches the place where better decisions most directly improve the work.

## Reporting for the people doing the work

Putting it together, building for the frontline means a shift in who reporting serves and how it is designed:

- **Recognize the gap.** Most reporting serves leadership and leaves the frontline without what it needs.
- **Start from the frontline's decisions**, which are concrete and immediate, not from leadership's summary view.
- **Make it immediate and specific**, answering "what should I do right now?" for this role, current and at a glance.
- **Aim it where the work happens**, because improving frontline decisions directly improves operations.

The goal is reporting that helps the people doing the work make better decisions, not just reporting that informs the people above them. Both matter, but the frontline is routinely neglected, and reaching it is where a lot of practical operational improvement lies. For an organization that has built all its reporting for the top, extending it to the frontline, with dashboards designed around the real, immediate decisions of the people doing the work, is often where the next real gains in how the organization operates are found. The people doing the work deserve reporting built for them, and the organization benefits most when they have it.
