Most conversations about reporting focus on producing insight: the right data, the right analysis, the report that surfaces the problem or the opportunity. But here is the uncomfortable truth: producing the insight is the **easy** part. The hard part, the part where the value actually lives, is getting someone to **act** on it. Reports that correctly identify problems no one does anything about are extremely common, and in those cases the insight, however real, produces **nothing.** This gap, from **insight to action**, is the last mile of reporting, and it is where most of the unrealized value sits. Here is why it matters and how to close it.

## Insight is only half the job

Start with the core point: the **value of reporting is realized only when an insight leads to an action**, not when the insight is **produced.** A report can do everything right, correct data, sound analysis, a real insight, a declining segment identified, a cost overrun caught, an opportunity surfaced, and if **nobody acts** on it, the business gets **no benefit.** The analysis was right and nothing changed.

This reframes what reporting is for. The point was never to **produce insights**; it was to **improve decisions and outcomes**, and insights only do that when they lead to action. An insight that sits in a report, unacted on, is like a correct diagnosis no one treats: accurate and useless. So producing insights is only **half** the job, and, importantly, the **easier** half. The half that actually delivers value, getting someone to **act**, is harder and far more often **neglected**, because the attention goes into producing the insight and stops once the insight exists, as if the job were done. It isn't. The job is done when something **changes** because of the insight, and that is a different and harder thing than producing it.

## Why insights go unacted on

If action is where the value is, why do so many insights go **unacted on?** Usually not because people are **unwilling** to act, but because the **last mile fails** in one of a few specific ways.

**The insight doesn't reach the right person.** It sits in a report that the people who could act on it don't read, or reaches them buried among many other things, so the person who could do something never really receives it. An insight delivered to the wrong audience, or to no one in particular, dies unacted.

**The insight isn't clear enough to act on.** It is there, but presented so its meaning or importance is not obvious, so even someone who sees it doesn't register what it means or that it matters. An insight that requires work to extract from the report often doesn't get extracted.

**The insight doesn't come with a clear next step.** It identifies a problem but gives no indication of the **action** it implies, so even someone who sees and understands it is left unsure what to **do**, and in the absence of a clear next step, often does nothing.

The common thread is that the gap is a **delivery and design** problem, not a **motivation** problem. People generally will act on a clear, relevant insight that reaches them with an obvious next step; they fail to act when the insight doesn't reach them, isn't clear, or doesn't point anywhere. So closing the gap is about **designing the reporting** to deliver insights in a way that leads to action, not about exhorting people to care more.

## Designing reporting that leads to action

Closing the insight-to-action gap means **designing reporting around decisions and actions**, not just around **displaying information.** Concretely, that means attending to the three failure points.

**Reach the right person.** Know **who can act** on each insight and get it to **them**, rather than producing a report and hoping the right person happens to see the relevant part. Reporting designed for action considers its **audience** and ensures insights reach the people positioned to act on them.

**Make the insight clear.** Present insights so their **meaning and implication are obvious**, not buried, so the person who receives it immediately grasps what it says and why it matters. This is where report design, clarity, emphasis, surfacing what matters, does real work: it is the difference between an insight that lands and one that is technically present but effectively invisible.

**Point to the next step.** Connect an insight to the **action it suggests**, so the next step is clear. This does not mean the report makes the decision, but that it surfaces the insight in a way that makes the implied action evident, so the person is not left wondering what to do with it.

Reporting built this way, **around what decisions it should drive and for whom**, closes the gap; reporting built only to **show information** leaves insights sitting there. The shift is from asking **"what information should we display?"** to asking **"what decisions should this drive, and who needs to act?"**, which changes how the whole thing is designed, from a display of data to a driver of action. That shift is the essence of reporting that actually produces value rather than just producing insights.

## Where the unrealized value sits

The practical significance of all this is that **most of the unrealized value in reporting sits in the insight-to-action gap.** Many organizations already have reporting that surfaces **real, useful insights**, and those insights **go nowhere**, so the value they could create is never realized. The limiting factor is usually **not a shortage of insight** but a shortage of **action** on the insight that already exists.

This has a clear implication: closing the gap, getting the insights you **already have** to actually drive action, often delivers **more** than producing **more** insights. If your reporting surfaces good insights that nobody acts on, adding more insights to the pile doesn't help; the problem is downstream of insight, in action. So the highest-value improvement is frequently not analytical, better analysis, more reports, but about the **last mile**: making the insights you produce reach the right people clearly with clear next steps, so they actually change what people **do.** This is why focusing on whether reporting actually **changes behavior**, not just whether it produces insight, is where a lot of the real, unrealized value lies, and why genuinely good reporting is designed to **close the gap**, not just to inform. A report that informs but changes nothing has done half the job; a report that drives action has done the whole one.

## The point in short

The value of reporting is in the action, not the insight:

- Reporting delivers value only when an insight leads to **action**, not when the insight is produced.
- Insights go **unacted on** when they don't reach the right person, aren't clear, or lack a next step.
- Closing the gap means **designing around decisions**: reach the right person, make it clear, point to the step.
- Most **unrealized value** sits in this gap, in good insights that go nowhere.

The throughline is that producing an insight is the easy and over-attended half of reporting, while getting someone to act on it is the hard and neglected half where the value actually lives. Reports that surface real problems no one acts on are common, and they represent enormous unrealized value, insight that never becomes outcome. Closing that last mile, by designing reporting to deliver insights to the right people, clearly, with clear next steps, is what turns reporting from something that informs into something that changes what the business does, which is the only thing that was ever the point.
