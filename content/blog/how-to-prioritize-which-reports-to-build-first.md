Every reporting program faces the same early question: with limited time and a long list of possible reports, which do you build first? Most answer it badly, working through a wish list in the order things were requested, or tackling whatever seems most impressive, and the result is effort spread across reports of wildly different value. There is a better way, and it is not complicated. Prioritize by the value of the decision each report improves against the cost to build it, and start where that ratio is best. Here is how, and why it matters more than it seems.

## Most roadmaps are wish lists

Start by naming the common failure. Most reporting roadmaps are **wish lists**, a collection of every report someone asked for, ordered by who asked most insistently or most recently. This feels responsive, you are building what people want, but it is a poor way to prioritize, because the volume and volume of a request have little to do with the value of the report.

The person who asks loudest is not necessarily asking for the most valuable report, and the report that would most improve the organization's decisions may be one nobody specifically requested, because the people who would benefit do not know it is possible. Building in request order means letting the squeakiest wheel set the priorities, which spreads effort across reports of very different impact and often puts low-value work first simply because someone pushed for it.

So the first step to prioritizing well is to stop treating the request list as the priority list. What people asked for is useful input, but it is not the ranking. The ranking has to come from value, which requests only loosely reflect.

## Rank by value and cost

The right basis for prioritizing is two things, assessed for each candidate report: the **value of the decision** it would improve, and the **cost to build** it.

The **value** comes from how important the decision is and how much better information would actually make it. A report that meaningfully improves a decision the business makes often, with real stakes, is high-value. A report that informs a minor or rare decision, or that would not change the decision much even with better data, is low-value, however interesting it looks. Assessing value means asking, honestly, what decision this report improves and how much the improvement is worth.

The **cost** comes from the data and the work involved. A report that can be built on data that is already clean and available is cheap; one that requires wrangling messy sources, building new data pipelines, or heavy modeling is expensive. Cost is not just effort but also time to value, how long before the report is delivering.

With both assessed, you rank by the **ratio**: the value of the decision against the cost to build. This is a simple, principled basis for sequencing that cuts through the wish-list approach and points at where the effort actually pays off most.

## Start where the ratio is best

The ranking tells you where to start: the reports with the **best value-to-cost ratio**, high-value decisions that are relatively cheap to serve. These are the ones to build first, and they are often not the ones instinct would pick.

Instinct tends toward two wrong starting points. One is the **hardest** report, the ambitious, impressive one, on the theory that it shows the most capability. But a high-cost report, even a valuable one, is a poor first choice, because it takes long to deliver and risks the program's early credibility on a difficult build. The other wrong start is the **most-requested** report, which, as above, reflects who asked loudest rather than what delivers most value.

The right start is the high-value, low-cost report: the decision that matters, served by data that is reasonably available, so it can be delivered quickly and clearly demonstrate value. Starting there means the program shows real impact early, on something that mattered, without a long or risky build. The hard and expensive reports can come later, once the program has proven itself and earned the time and trust to tackle them. Sequencing by value-to-cost puts the quick, valuable wins first and defers the costly work until it is better supported.

## Early wins carry the program

The reason getting the first reports right matters so much is that a reporting program runs on **trust and momentum**, and the early reports set both. This is often underappreciated: the first few reports are not just deliverables, they are what the whole program's credibility rests on.

When the first reports delivered are high-value and clearly useful, people see the value quickly, trust in the program grows, and support strengthens, which makes everything after it easier, more cooperation, more patience for the harder work, more willingness to invest further. When the early efforts are low-impact, slow, or disappointing, the program loses credibility before it has proven anything, and every later report faces more skepticism and less support.

So prioritizing for early wins is not only about those specific reports; it is about setting up the entire program to succeed. A program that proves its value early, with high-value reports delivered quickly, earns the momentum to grow. One that starts slow or low-impact struggles to recover. This is why the value-to-cost ranking, which naturally surfaces the quick, valuable wins, is also the sequencing that most helps the program as a whole, not just the individual reports.

## Prioritizing keeps the program focused

Beyond sequencing, deliberate prioritization does something else important: it keeps the program **focused** rather than letting it sprawl. Reporting programs sprawl when they try to satisfy every request, building whatever anyone asks for, which spreads effort thin, dilutes value, and produces an ever-growing backlog nobody can finish.

A clear prioritization gives you a principled basis for saying no, or not yet, to the low-value requests, without it feeling arbitrary or political. When a request comes in, you assess its decision value and build cost, rank it, and build it when it reaches the top, rather than adding it to an undifferentiated pile. This lets the program decline or defer low-value work on a clear basis, keeping the effort pointed at what delivers value rather than at satisfying every voice. The discipline of building in priority order, and not just accumulating requested reports, is what keeps a reporting program a focused value-delivery effort rather than a sprawling backlog.

## A simple way to prioritize

Putting it together, prioritizing which reports to build first comes down to a repeatable approach:

- **Stop using the request list as the priority list.** What people asked for is input, not the ranking.
- **Assess each candidate's decision value**, how important the decision is and how much better information would improve it.
- **Assess each candidate's build cost**, the data and work involved and the time to value.
- **Rank by the value-to-cost ratio** and start where it is best, high-value decisions that are relatively cheap to serve.
- **Favor early wins**, because they build the trust and momentum the whole program needs.
- **Use the ranking to stay focused**, declining or deferring low-value requests on a clear basis.

Done this way, a reporting program delivers value early, builds the credibility to tackle the harder work, and stays focused on impact rather than sprawling to satisfy every request. The alternative, working through a wish list in the order things were asked, spreads effort across reports of wildly different value and often spends the crucial early effort on the wrong things. Prioritizing by decision value against build cost is a simple discipline, but it is the difference between a reporting program that proves its worth and grows and one that struggles to show value and loses support. Where you start determines a great deal, so start where the value is highest and the cost is lowest, and let the rest follow.
