An accounting or bookkeeping firm is a business that sells its people's time, and it runs on a few numbers: how its capacity is used, how much of the time worked turns into revenue, and which clients are actually profitable. These are hard to see clearly because the data lives in time, billing, and workflow systems that do not reconcile, and the need to see them is sharpest exactly when the firm is most stretched, during busy season. Power BI's role for an accounting firm is to join those systems into one governed view, so capacity, realization, and client profitability are visible, including when the firm most needs them. This is how firms use it.

## The numbers are split across systems

Ask an accounting firm a question that should be simple, where is our capacity stretched, what is our realization, and which clients make money, and watch how hard it is to answer. Time is in the time system. Billing and collections are in billing. Workflow and status are in a practice management tool. Costs are in finance. Each holds a piece, none reconciles with the others, and the real picture of the firm has to be assembled by hand.

The first and most valuable thing Power BI does is build a **governed model that joins these systems**, so capacity, realization, and client profitability can be read together by staff, client, and service. Power BI reads from the time, billing, and workflow systems and the finances, and the model relates them into one coherent picture. Once that model exists, the questions that used to take manual assembly become reports the firm opens. That join is the foundation, because every number that matters to a firm spans the systems, and no single one holds the whole answer.

## Capacity, especially in busy season

The number that most repays attention, and that is hardest to see when it matters, is **capacity**, and the moment it matters most is **busy season.** When the firm is slammed with returns and deadlines, it is most stretched and least able to assemble reports by hand, and it is exactly then that seeing where the work and the strain are concentrated is most valuable.

A governed model, refreshed frequently, makes capacity visible in real time: where work is piling up, which staff are overloaded, how the pipeline of returns or work is flowing. That lets managers rebalance work, redirect capacity, and spot problems before they become crises, while there is still time to act. During the period when the firm is under the most pressure and the stakes of mismanaging capacity are highest, having a current, clear view of where everything stands is often where the analytics most earns its place. A firm that can see its capacity during busy season manages the crunch; one assembling reports by hand in the middle of it is flying blind exactly when it can least afford to.

## Realization and client profitability

The numbers that determine whether the work was worth doing are **realization** and **client profitability**, and both are hidden when the data is scattered. Realization, the gap between time recorded and cash collected, is where firms quietly lose money they earned, through time written down, discounted, or never billed. Client profitability depends on the time a client actually consumes against what they pay, which is invisible when time and billing live apart.

A governed model joins the time, billing, and cost data so realization and client profitability become readable by client, service, and staff. That routinely surfaces the uncomfortable truth: the clients and work types that look fine on fees but lose money once the time is counted, the services that are systematically under-billed, the realization leaking away unseen. These insights change how a firm prices and staffs its work, which is where much of its margin is decided. For a firm that sells time, seeing which work and which clients genuinely make money, rather than which merely generate fees, is where the strongest decisions get made.

## Everyone sees their own scope

A firm has partners and managers who should see different things, and **row-level security** in the model handles it: a partner sees their clients and book, a manager sees their team and engagements, firm leadership sees everything, all from the same governed reports, with the rules defined once and inherited everywhere. That lets one model serve the whole firm, from an individual's utilization up to firm-wide realization, without building separate reports per role or worrying about who sees which client's economics.

## It scales down to smaller firms

The pain of assembling these numbers by hand falls hardest on **smaller firms**, where the work lands on partners and managers who should be serving clients, with no analyst to absorb it. A focused Power BI model over the time, billing, and workflow systems gives a small or midsize firm the same clear view of capacity, realization, and profitability that larger firms build teams to produce, without the overhead. You start with the few views that run the firm and grow from there, and because the underlying problem, scattered data that does not reconcile, is identical at any size, the approach scales down well. For a smaller firm that relies on these numbers but cannot staff for them, getting the capability without the team is exactly what fits.

## What accounting firms end up with

An accounting or bookkeeping firm running on Power BI ends up with:

- Time, billing, and workflow data joined so capacity, realization, and profitability can be read together.
- Capacity visible in real time, especially through busy season, so work and strain can be rebalanced before they become crises.
- Realization and client profitability surfaced, so the firm sees where it earns and where it quietly loses money.
- Each partner and manager seeing their own scope through row-level security, from one model.

The throughline is that an accounting firm stops assembling its numbers by hand from systems that disagree, hardest of all during the busy season when it has least time, and starts running on one governed view where capacity, realization, and profitability are clear and current. The firm manages the crunch with its eyes open, sees the work that quietly loses money, and prices and staffs on evidence. For a firm that sells its people's time and lives or dies on how well it manages capacity and realization, having those numbers be clear, current, and produced for it rather than squeezed out by hand is what turns the time and billing data the firm already captures into a firm that is run on information, especially when it matters most.
