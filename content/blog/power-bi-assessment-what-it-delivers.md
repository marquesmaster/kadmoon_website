The hardest part of a Power BI project is often the beginning, because that is where the biggest decisions get made with the least information. A company commits to a scope, a budget, and a timeline before anyone has looked closely at the data, and both sides end up guessing. The assessment exists to replace that guess with evidence. This is what it is, what you get, and why it is the right way to start.

## What a Power BI assessment actually is

A Power BI assessment is a **short, fixed-scope first project**. It is not a discovery call, a free proposal, or a demo of someone else's data. Over about two weeks, a senior team connects a slice of your real data, builds a first governed model, and ships working dashboards for one priority area of your business.

The word that matters is **working**. You end the assessment with reports built on your own numbers, running in your own Microsoft tenant, that a leader can actually open and use. You do not end it with a slide deck of recommendations. That difference is the whole point: it turns an abstract conversation about analytics into something concrete you can judge.

## The three things it does in two weeks

An assessment is deliberately narrow so that it can go deep. It focuses on one priority area, finance, sales, operations, whatever decision matters most right now, and does three things well.

### It maps the decisions

Before touching the data, the team names the decisions your leaders actually need to make and the questions behind them. Analytics earns its keep when it changes a decision, so the work starts there: what does a controller, a sales leader, or an operations lead need to see, how often, and what will they do differently once they see it. This keeps the output pointed at something useful instead of whatever data happened to be easy to chart.

### It connects a slice of your real data

The team connects to the real source systems for the priority area, your ERP, CRM, finance system, or database, and pulls the data the decisions depend on. Working with real data, rather than a sanitized sample, is what surfaces the actual condition of your data: where it is clean, where it is messy, and what it will take to make it trustworthy. That knowledge is what makes the eventual build plan accurate.

### It builds a first governed model and dashboards

On that data, the team builds a first **governed semantic model**, measures defined once, a clean structure, and the dashboards that answer the priority questions. Even in two weeks, this is built the right way, on a model rather than wired straight to raw tables, so it is a real foundation and not a throwaway prototype.

## What you walk away with

At the end of the assessment you have four things:

- **Working Power BI dashboards** on your own data, in your own tenant.
- **A first governed model** for the priority area, built to extend.
- **A clear architecture and delivery plan** for the full build.
- **A fixed-price quote**, grounded in what your data actually looks like rather than a generic estimate.

That last point is where the assessment earns its cost. A quote produced after two weeks inside your real data is accurate in a way a quote produced from a questionnaire never is. It is not padded to cover unknowns, because the unknowns have been looked at.

## Why starting small de-risks the whole thing

The assessment is really a risk-management tool, and it works for both sides.

For you, it means you see **real value before you commit** to a larger engagement. You are not betting a budget on a promise. You are deciding whether to continue based on dashboards you can already use and a plan you can already read. If the approach does not fit, you have lost two weeks and gained working reports for one area, not a quarter and a large invoice.

For the team building it, the assessment means the larger scope is based on evidence instead of optimism. Traditional projects go over budget because they are scoped before anyone has seen the data, and the surprises all land later. An assessment front-loads the surprises into a two-week window where they are cheap to handle.

This is also why the assessment is **paid**. Free proposals are guesses, and a guess is worth what you pay for it. Paid work means the team is engaged in solving your problem, and it means the output, working reports and an accurate plan, is genuinely yours.

## Where it leads: not a third model

It helps to be clear about where the assessment sits. It is a **paid on-ramp**, a short first project, not a standing engagement model of its own. After it, you choose how to continue:

- **By project.** A fixed-scope build with defined deliverables, acceptance criteria, and milestone billing. Right when the outcome is well defined.
- **Managed Power BI.** A monthly subscription where a senior team runs and evolves your Power BI, refreshes, governance, new reports, as the business changes. Right for ongoing work and a partner on call.

Many clients start with a project and move to Managed Power BI after go-live. Either way, everything from the assessment forward is built in your own Microsoft tenant, documented, and handed over, so you own it regardless of how you choose to continue, or whether you continue at all.

## Who it is for

An assessment is the right starting point if you recognize any of these:

- Your teams walk into meetings with different numbers and spend the first ten minutes arguing about which is right.
- A simple question takes a week because someone has to re-key exports by hand.
- You have Power BI already, but it grew into a sprawl of workspaces nobody governs.
- You want to invest in analytics but cannot get an honest, specific scope out of anyone.

In each case, the fastest way to make the problem concrete is to put real dashboards on your real data and see what it takes. That is what the two weeks buy you: not a recommendation about analytics, but the first working piece of it, and an honest map of the rest.
