A moving or relocation company runs on estimates, crews, and seasons. It prices jobs on estimates, delivers them with labor-heavy crews, and sees demand swing dramatically with the time of year. Its margin is decided by whether estimates hold, how productively crews work, and how well it plans for seasonal demand, and seeing all of that clearly is hard, because booking, dispatch, and financial data live in separate systems. Power BI's role is to join those systems into one governed view, so estimate accuracy, crew productivity, and seasonal demand are visible where the margin is actually made. This is how moving companies use it.

## The margin drivers live in separate systems

Ask a moving company the questions that decide its results, are our estimates holding up, are crews productive, and how is demand trending, and the answer takes someone pulling from booking and dispatch systems, adding financials, and assembling it by hand. Booking and estimating software holds the quotes, dispatch holds the crews and jobs, finance holds the money, and none of it joins into a view of where margin is made. The company sees the operational picture but not clearly whether estimates held, which crews are productive, or how demand compares to past seasons, all of which decide its profitability.

The first and most valuable thing Power BI does is build a **governed model that joins these systems**, so estimate accuracy, crew productivity, job margin, and demand can be read together by job, crew, and over time. Power BI reads from the booking, dispatch, and financial sources, and the model relates them into one coherent picture. Once that model exists, the questions about estimates, crews, and demand become reports the company opens. That join is the foundation, because the business's margin depends on connecting estimates to actuals to crews to revenue, and no single system shows that whole.

## Estimate accuracy: where the margin is set

A moving job is priced on an **estimate**, which makes **estimate accuracy** a direct driver of margin: a job that runs **over** its estimated time or resources quietly loses the margin the quote assumed. The estimate is the basis of the price, so when actual hours, crew size, or truck time exceed it, the job earns less than planned, and when that happens routinely, margin erodes with no obvious single cause, just a persistent gap between what jobs were quoted to earn and what they did.

A governed model that compares **estimated against actual by job** makes that gap visible. The company can see where estimates are consistently off, which **job types or estimators** tend to run over, and the patterns behind the overruns, so it can tighten estimating and price accurately. This is central to moving economics, because the whole pricing model rests on estimates being right, and a company that cannot see estimate accuracy is pricing on guesses it never checks. Seeing estimated versus actual turns estimating from a hopeful exercise into a **measured** one, protecting the margin that the estimates are supposed to secure. A company that closes this loop prices with confidence; one that does not watches margin leak through overruns it cannot locate.

## Crew productivity: the operational lever

Moving is **labor-driven**, so **crew productivity** is the main operational lever and a major factor in margin, and the governed view makes it visible. The spread between efficient and inefficient crews directly affects the bottom line, because labor and time are the main costs, but that spread is hard to see when the data sits in dispatch software built to schedule jobs, not to measure how productively they get done.

A governed model that shows **productivity by crew**, jobs completed and revenue produced against the hours and cost, lets a company see which crews produce, where time is lost, and how crews compare. With that visible, it can improve scheduling, address underperformance, and learn from the best crews, lifting productivity across the operation. For a business whose margin is made in the field one crew at a time, seeing productivity clearly is directly seeing where the business is made, and managing it deliberately is one of the highest-leverage things the company can do, because a modest improvement in crew productivity flows straight to margin on a cost base that is mostly labor and time.

## Seasonality: planning for the peak

Moving demand is **extremely seasonal**, concentrated in peak months, so a company has to plan staffing, trucks, and capacity **ahead**, and what matters is not just current bookings but how they compare to the **same point in prior seasons.** That comparison tells the company whether demand is running ahead or behind, which is what lets it staff and plan in time rather than being caught short in peak or overstaffed in the lulls.

A governed model that keeps **booking history and makes it comparable across seasons** reveals this. The company can see demand developing against prior years and plan capacity accordingly, scaling up for a strong peak, trimming for a soft one, with enough lead time to actually act. For a business with such pronounced seasonality, where being understaffed in peak means turned-away work and being overstaffed in the lull means carried cost, seeing demand against history is central to planning well, because the planning decisions must be made before the season arrives and are far better made on the evidence of how this year compares to past years than on guesswork. Seeing the season coming is what lets the company meet it rather than react to it.

## It fits companies of any size

A smaller moving company benefits as clearly as a large one, because even a modest operation runs jobs, estimates, crews, and seasonal demand whose data lives in separate systems reconciled by hand. Bringing it into one governed view gives a smaller company a clear picture of estimate accuracy, crew productivity, and demand without a data team, through a partner who builds it and a managed arrangement that keeps it running. Larger companies run more crews and trucks, but the core value, seeing estimates, productivity, and seasonal demand clearly, applies at any size, and the underlying economics are the same whether you run a few crews or many.

## What moving companies end up with

A moving or relocation company running on Power BI ends up with:

- Booking, dispatch, and financial data joined so the margin drivers can be read together.
- Estimate accuracy visible as estimated versus actual, so overruns and mispricing become clear.
- Crew productivity visible, so the main operational lever can be managed.
- Seasonal demand seen against prior years, so capacity is planned in time.

The throughline is that a moving company stops piecing its margin drivers together by hand and starts seeing estimate accuracy, crew productivity, and seasonal demand on one governed view, where the business is actually decided. Estimates get checked against reality and tightened, crew productivity becomes an operational lever the company can pull, and seasonal demand gets planned for in time. For a business that prices on estimates, delivers with crews, and lives through dramatic seasons, seeing all three clearly is what turns the scattered booking and dispatch data into the decisions that protect margin and meet demand when it comes.
