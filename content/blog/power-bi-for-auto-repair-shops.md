An independent auto repair shop lives on a handful of operating numbers: how many cars it services, how much each one is worth, how productively its technicians work, and how its margin splits between parts and labor. These are the numbers that run the shop, and seeing them clearly is harder than it should be, because work order, technician, and financial data sit in the shop management system and financials, and owners often end up reading month-end totals rather than managing live operating numbers. Power BI's role is to join those systems into one governed view, so car count, average repair order, and technician productivity are visible where the shop's numbers are actually made. This is how auto repair shops use it.

## Running on totals instead of operating numbers

Ask a shop owner the numbers that should run the business day to day, what is our car count trending, how is our average repair order, which technicians are productive, and the answer often comes from the shop management system's reports read at month end, as totals, without the trends and breakdowns that would make them actionable. The management system holds work orders, technicians, and customers; finance holds the money; and the operating numbers that matter are there but not brought together over time in a way an owner can manage by. The shop runs on **month-end totals** rather than **live operating numbers**, which means problems and opportunities are seen late.

The first and most valuable thing Power BI does is build a **governed model that joins these systems**, so car count, average repair order, technician productivity, and the parts-versus-labor split can be read together by technician, service type, and over time. Power BI reads from the shop management system and financials, and the model relates them into one coherent picture. Once that model exists, the operating numbers become reports the owner opens, current and broken down, rather than totals at month end. That join is the foundation, because running a shop well depends on seeing these numbers clearly and in time, and the raw system reports rarely show them that way.

## Car count and average repair order: the two revenue levers

A repair shop's revenue is driven by two numbers that are best understood **together**: **car count**, how many vehicles the shop services, and **average repair order**, the average revenue per vehicle. Revenue is essentially car count times average repair order, so the shop grows by bringing in **more cars** or by growing **each ticket**, and knowing which lever to pull is central to running the shop.

A governed model that tracks **both over time** shows which lever the shop should focus on. A shop with strong car count but a low average repair order has room to grow each ticket, through thorough inspections, clearly presented recommended work, and capturing the service a car actually needs. A shop with a healthy average repair order but thin car count needs to grow **traffic**, through marketing, retention, and reputation. Seeing both together is what makes this clear, because looking at revenue alone hides which half is strong and which is the opportunity. The owner can see, for instance, that car count is fine but the average ticket lags the shop's potential, and focus the team on the inspection-and-recommendation process that grows it, rather than chasing more cars the shop may not need. The two numbers together turn "we want to grow revenue" into a specific, answerable question about **which lever**, which is far more useful than the total alone.

## Technician productivity and the parts-labor split

Where the shop's **margin** is actually decided is in **technician productivity** and the **parts-versus-labor split**, and the governed view makes both visible. Technicians produce the labor that drives much of the shop's profit, and the spread in productivity between them affects the bottom line directly, a productive tech turns more billable hours out of the same day. The parts-versus-labor split shapes margin because parts and labor carry different economics, and the balance between them affects how profitable the shop's work actually is.

A governed model that shows **productivity by technician** and **margin by parts and labor** lets an owner manage what drives the margin. It can see who is producing and who needs support, where labor hours are being used well or lost, and how the margin breaks down between parts and labor across service types, so the shop can improve scheduling, lift productivity, and price appropriately. This turns the shop's margin from a **month-end result** the owner discovers into something **managed day to day**, by watching the numbers that drive it and acting on them in time. For a shop where the difference between a good month and a poor one often comes down to how productively the bays ran and how the work mixed between parts and labor, seeing these clearly is the core of managing the business rather than just totaling it up afterward.

## It fits shops of any size

A single-location shop benefits as clearly as a multi-shop group, because even one shop generates work order, technician, and financial data that gets read as month-end totals rather than managed as live operating numbers. Bringing it into one governed view gives a single shop a clear picture of car count, average repair order, and technician productivity without a data team, through a partner who builds it and a managed arrangement that keeps it running. Multi-shop groups gain the ability to compare locations and spot which shops run best, but the core value, seeing the real operating numbers clearly and in time, applies to a single shop just as much, because the need to run on operating numbers rather than month-end totals is the same at any size.

## What auto repair shops end up with

An auto repair shop running on Power BI ends up with:

- Shop management and financial data joined so the real operating numbers can be read over time.
- Car count and average repair order seen together, so the right growth lever becomes clear.
- Technician productivity and the parts-labor split visible, so margin is managed, not just totaled.

For a business whose results come down to how many cars it serves, how much each is worth, and how productively the bays run, seeing those numbers clearly and in time is what turns the shop management data into the decisions that grow revenue and protect margin, month after month.
