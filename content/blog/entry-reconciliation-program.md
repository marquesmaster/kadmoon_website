Not every number on an import entry can honestly be final when the entry is filed. Sometimes the price is subject to an adjustment that will not be settled until after year-end. Sometimes a preference claim is correct but the documentation proving it is not yet complete. In those situations the importer faces a bad choice between guessing and leaving an error, or holding up the entry, unless it uses the mechanism built for exactly this timing gap. Reconciliation lets you declare an estimate now and settle the true figure later, on purpose and on the record. The declaring is the easy part; the tracking of what remains open is where it succeeds or fails, and that is a data problem.

## Some values are genuinely not final at entry

The premise of reconciliation is honest uncertainty. There are elements of an entry, particularly [value](/blog/customs-value-valuation), that may not be known at the time of entry through no fault of the importer. A related-party price set under a transfer-pricing policy may be adjusted after the fact. A [preference claim](/blog/usmca-2026-review-importers) may be genuinely valid while the proof is still being assembled. In each case the truthful position at entry is an estimate, and the real figure arrives later. Pretending otherwise, by guessing a final number, creates an error; delaying the entry is not practical. Reconciliation exists so that the estimate can be declared openly with a formal path to correct it.

## Declare the estimate, flag the entry

The mechanism works by flagging the entry as subject to later correction and then settling it once the true values are determined. This turns what would otherwise be an unresolved inaccuracy into a managed, declared position: Customs knows the figure is an estimate, the importer has committed to correcting it, and there is a defined route to do so. It sits within the same [accuracy discipline](/blog/duty-optimization-four-levers) as classification and valuation, handling the specific case where accuracy at entry is impossible but accuracy overall is still required. Used properly, it is the responsible way to handle a value that cannot yet be final, not a loophole.

## Open items are money and obligation

A flagged entry is not finished; it is an open item carrying either a liability or a refund, waiting to be resolved. If the final figure is higher than the estimate, there is more duty owed; if lower, there may be a refund due. Either way the item sits open until it is settled, and an unsettled reconciliation is both a financial position that is not yet closed and a compliance obligation that has not yet been met. Leaving such items untracked is how an importer ends up with unresolved exposure it has lost sight of, or forgone refunds it never claimed, the same way untracked [liquidation](/blog/entry-liquidation-tracking) leaves money and deadlines on the table.

## The follow-through is the hard part

The declaration is straightforward; the follow-through is where reconciliation lives or dies, and the follow-through is entirely a tracking problem. It means knowing which entries are flagged, what each is waiting on, when each must be settled, and whether the resolving information has arrived, across time and after the operational urgency of each entry has long passed. These are open items with deadlines, scattered through the calendar, and they are exactly the kind of low-urgency, long-horizon obligation that manual processes let slip because nothing prompts a review. [Exception-based](/blog/exception-management-trade) analytics handles this naturally, holding the flagged entries in view and surfacing each deadline before it passes.
