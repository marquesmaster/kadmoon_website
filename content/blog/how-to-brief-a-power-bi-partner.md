The difference between a Power BI project that lands and one that disappoints is often decided before any work starts, in how the engagement is briefed. A partner can only build what they understand, and the brief is where that understanding is formed. Yet most briefs start in the wrong place, with a list of dashboards someone imagines they want, which quietly locks in an answer before anyone has understood the question. Here is how to brief a Power BI partner so the project actually goes well, from someone who has seen both the good briefs and the ones that caused trouble.

## Lead with decisions, not dashboards

The single most important thing to get right is where the brief starts. Start with the **decisions you want to make better**, not the charts you think you want.

This feels backward to many people, because a dashboard is concrete and a decision is abstract, so it is tempting to open with "I want a sales dashboard with these five charts." But a report built from a list of charts is a report built from a guess about what will help, and guesses are often wrong. A report built backward from a real decision, who makes it, how often, what they would do differently with better information, is built to actually help, because it was aimed at a need rather than assembled from available data.

So the most valuable thing you can bring to the conversation is clarity about the decisions: which ones matter, who owns them, and what is hard about them today. A good partner takes that and works out the charts, the layout, and the model themselves, because those are the answer, not the question. If you come in with the charts already specified, you have answered the question before anyone understood it.

## Name your sources, and be honest about them

The second thing that prevents most trouble is being straight about your data early. Name the actual systems the relevant data lives in, and be honest about their state.

This matters because partners plan completely differently for different data realities. Clean data in a few cloud systems is one kind of project; a tangle of legacy systems, inconsistent definitions, and spreadsheets that quietly hold critical logic is another. The surprises that derail Power BI projects are almost always data realities that surfaced too late, a source that turned out to be messier than anyone said, a key number that lived in someone's personal spreadsheet, a system that could not be connected the way everyone assumed.

You are not expected to have perfect data. Almost nobody does, and a good partner expects to deal with reality. What you are expected to do is be honest about the state of it, including the embarrassing parts, so the plan is built on the real situation rather than an optimistic version of it. The messy spreadsheet you are tempted not to mention is exactly the thing worth mentioning.

## Say who the reports are for

The third thing to make clear is the audience, because a report for an executive and a report for an analyst are different objects even when they cover the same data.

An executive report answers a few important questions on sight, prioritizes clarity over depth, and is read in seconds between other things. An analyst's report supports exploration, carries more detail, and rewards digging in. A report for a frontline operator is built around the specific decisions of that role. If the partner does not know who the report is for and how they will use it, they are designing blind, and the result often fits nobody well because it was aimed at an average user who does not exist.

So tell the partner, for each thing being built, who uses it and how. That single piece of context shapes the design more than almost anything else, and it is cheap to provide and expensive to omit.

## Agree what good looks like

The fourth thing, and the one most often skipped, is agreeing upfront what a successful outcome actually looks like, in terms that matter.

The weak version of success is deliverables shipped: the dashboards were built and handed over. That can be true while the project failed, if nobody uses them. The real version of success is about **decisions and adoption**: the right people are using the reports to make the decisions the project was meant to improve. Agreeing on that definition at the start changes how the whole engagement is run, because it keeps everyone pointed at the outcome rather than the output.

This also gives you and the partner a shared basis for judging the work. A report that looks impressive but goes unused has not succeeded against a decisions-and-adoption standard, and setting that standard upfront makes it legitimate to say so and fix it, rather than declaring victory at handover and discovering failure months later.

## Expect pushback, and welcome it

One last thing worth knowing: a good Power BI partner will **push back** on a brief that starts from dashboards, and will ask about decisions, audiences, and data instead. This is not the partner being difficult or avoiding your request. It is the partner doing the most important part of the job, which is understanding the problem before building the solution.

Welcome that conversation. A partner who takes a dashboard list and simply builds it, without asking what decisions it serves or who uses it, is easier in the moment and more likely to deliver something that disappoints. A partner who interrogates the brief is doing the work that makes the project succeed. The best engagements start with that back-and-forth, where the partner helps you turn a wish list into a clear picture of the decisions, the users, and the data, and then builds against that. If you come in expecting and valuing that conversation rather than resisting it, you are already most of the way to a project that goes well.

## A short brief that works

If you want a simple structure to brief against, this covers most of what matters:

- **The decisions:** which business decisions should get better, and who makes them.
- **The users:** who will use each report, and how.
- **The data:** where it really lives, and honestly what state it is in.
- **Success:** what adoption and better decisions would look like in a few months.
- **The constraints:** your budget and timeline, shared as design inputs.

Bring that, expect the partner to probe it, and you have given a Power BI engagement the thing it most needs to succeed: a clear understanding of the problem, before anyone starts building the solution.
