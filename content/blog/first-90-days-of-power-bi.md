One of the most common questions before starting with Power BI is also one of the hardest to get a straight answer to: what actually happens, and how long until it is worth something? The honest picture is more reassuring and more disciplined than either extreme people imagine, the instant-dashboard fantasy or the year-long platform project. Good Power BI starts small, proves value fast, and builds from there. Here is a realistic look at what the first 90 days actually involve, and what you should expect to have at the end of them.

## Start small, prove value fast

The single most important thing to understand about how good Power BI engagements run is the shape: **start small and prove value fast**, rather than disappear into a long build that delivers nothing until a distant launch.

The long-build approach is tempting because it feels thorough, but it is high-risk: you invest for months before learning whether the result works, and if the scope was wrong, you find out at the end when it is expensive to fix. The better approach scopes a focused first project that reaches real users within weeks, so value and learning start early and compound. You will not have a complete estate in 90 days, and you should not expect to. You should expect a working, trusted, adopted first deliverable and the foundation the rest builds on. That shape, early value compounding rather than a big-bang finish, is what the first 90 days are designed around.

## Weeks 1 to 3: assessment and understanding

The first weeks go into **understanding**, not visible dashboards, and this can feel slow to anyone expecting immediate charts. It is deliberate, because this is where the project is aimed correctly.

It starts with an assessment that turns a vague goal, we want better reporting, into a clear picture: which decisions the reporting should serve, who makes them, how they are made today, and what state the underlying data is actually in. This is also where the data reality surfaces, honestly, the clean sources and the messy ones, before they become mid-project surprises. The output is a focused plan: a specific first build aimed at a real decision, scoped to deliver value quickly rather than to boil the ocean.

This assessment phase is the paid on-ramp that sets everything up, and shortchanging it is how projects go wrong, because a build aimed at the wrong decision is thorough and useless. Spending the first weeks getting the aim right is what makes the rest of the 90 days productive.

## Weeks 3 to 7: the governed model and first reports

With the aim set, the next stretch goes into the **governed model** and the first reports built on it. This is the core of the work, and much of it is invisible, which is worth understanding so the progress is not misread.

The model comes first because it is what makes every report trustworthy. Defining the metrics once, relating the data properly, building in the security, this is the foundation that makes the numbers consistent and the reports reliable. It does not produce a dashboard to look at on day one, but it is what prevents the disagreeing-numbers nightmare later. Rushing past it to show a quick dashboard produces something that looks like progress and breaks trust when the numbers do not hold up.

On that model, the first reports take shape, aimed at the real decision the assessment identified. They are built to target that decision specifically, to answer its question on sight, and to reach the actual users who make it. The goal is not comprehensiveness but a focused, trustworthy first deliverable that does a real job.

## Weeks 7 to 12: reaching real adoption

The final stretch is about getting the first reports **used**, because adoption, not delivery, is what creates value. A report that ships and is not used has returned nothing, so this phase is as important as the build.

That means getting the reports in front of the intended users, connected to how they actually work, and establishing trust by showing that the numbers reconcile with what they already believe. It means watching whether the reports are actually opened and used in real decisions, and adjusting based on what you learn, because the first version is rarely perfect and the feedback is valuable. Starting focused, with a specific audience and decision, is what makes this manageable: you are earning adoption with one working example rather than pushing a big launch to everyone and hoping it sticks.

By the end of this phase, the aim is a first deliverable that is genuinely adopted, trusted, and improving a real decision, which is the thing that proves the whole approach and builds the credibility to expand.

## What you should have at 90 days

At the end of the first 90 days, a well-run engagement has produced:

- A **governed model** that makes the numbers consistent and trustworthy, which the rest of the estate will build on.
- A **first set of reports** targeting a real decision, built on that model.
- **Actual adoption**, those reports used by the intended audience, with trust established because the numbers reconcile.
- A **clear path forward**: which decisions and audiences to serve next, what data work is needed, and how to govern the growth.

What you should not expect is a finished estate covering everything, and that is by design. The goal of the first 90 days is a solid, proven foundation and real momentum, a working example that is adopted and trusted, which is worth far more than a large but unused estate delivered all at once. From that foundation, the estate grows on the strength of something that already works, which is exactly how Power BI is supposed to be built: not in one big launch, but in proven steps that compound. The first 90 days are about earning the first of those steps, and setting up all the ones after it.
