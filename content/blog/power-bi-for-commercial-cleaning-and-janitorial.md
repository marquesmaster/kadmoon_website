A commercial cleaning or janitorial company is a labor business running contracts: it bids a price to clean a site and makes its margin on whether the labor to deliver that service stays below the price. Its profitability is decided contract by contract, and seeing that clearly is hard, because labor, scheduling, and financial data live in separate systems, and the company tends to see totals, what it billed and what it spent, without seeing which contracts actually make money. Power BI's role is to join those systems into one governed view, so labor cost, contract margin, and cost to serve are visible across every account. This is how commercial cleaning companies use it.

## Profitability hides below the total

Ask a cleaning company a question that should drive the business, which of our contracts actually make money, and the answer is usually hard to give, because the company sees its overall labor cost and overall revenue but not how they break down to each account. Scheduling and workforce systems hold the hours, time and attendance holds what was worked, finance holds the revenue, and none of it joins into a contract-level view. The company knows it is profitable or not **in total**, but not **where**, which means the accounts quietly losing money are hidden inside a company result that may look fine.

The first and most valuable thing Power BI does is build a **governed model that joins these systems**, so labor hours, cost, and revenue can be read together by account, site, and over time. Power BI reads from scheduling, time, and financial sources, and the model relates them into one coherent picture. Once that model exists, the company can see cost and revenue at the level where profitability is actually decided. That join is the foundation, because the business's profitability lives below the total, at the contract level, and no single system connects the labor on each account to the revenue it brings.

## Cost to serve: where margin is decided

The number that matters is **cost to serve**: what it actually costs to deliver service to a given contract or site, which in cleaning is dominated by **labor**, the hours and wages spent there. This is where profitability is decided, because the economics vary from one contract to another. A well-priced account cleaned efficiently makes good margin; an account where the labor crept above what it was priced for loses money, and the company total hides both.

A governed model that joins **labor to revenue by account** lets a company see cost to serve against contract value, so it can tell which accounts pay and which do not. With that visible, real decisions become possible: repricing contracts whose labor no longer fits their value, addressing the staffing or efficiency on accounts that overrun, and knowing which contracts to keep, renegotiate, or let go. Without cost to serve, these are guesses; with it, they rest on what each contract actually costs to deliver. For a business where the same total margin can hide a mix of strongly profitable and quietly loss-making contracts, seeing cost to serve is the core of what the analytics provides, because it turns one company-level number into a map of where the money is actually made and lost.

## Labor: the cost that decides everything

Because **labor is the dominant cost** in cleaning, controlling it against each contract's revenue is where the margin is protected, and the governed view makes that possible. A contract where hours creep above what it was priced for quietly loses money, and when labor is seen only as a company total, that creep is invisible until it has already eroded overall margin, with no way to see which accounts caused it.

A governed model that shows **labor by account against contract value** surfaces exactly where the problems are: where hours are running high relative to revenue, where overtime concentrates, which sites are staffed beyond what they were priced for. With that visible, the company can act deliberately, adjusting staffing and scheduling on the accounts that overrun, addressing overtime where it clusters, and repricing where the labor genuinely exceeds the contract value, rather than watching overall margin slip with no idea where it is going. For a business whose single biggest cost is labor and whose margin is the gap between that labor and the contract price, seeing labor against revenue account by account is the heart of running it well, because it is the one place the margin is actually won or lost, and the company total shows none of it.

## It fits companies of any size

A smaller cleaning company benefits as clearly as a large one, because even a modest operation runs contracts whose labor and revenue live in separate systems reconciled by hand, and contract-level profitability is just as hidden. Bringing it into one governed view gives a smaller company a clear picture of which contracts pay, without a data team, through a partner who builds it and a managed arrangement that keeps it running. Larger companies run more accounts and staff, but the core value, cost to serve and contract margin, applies at any size, and the underlying problem, profitability hidden at the contract level, is the same whether you clean a few buildings or hundreds.

## What commercial cleaning companies end up with

A commercial cleaning or janitorial company running on Power BI ends up with:

- Scheduling, time, and financial data joined so cost to serve can be read at the contract level.
- Cost to serve visible against contract value, so which accounts pay becomes clear.
- Labor seen by account against revenue, so overruns show up where they actually hurt.
- A clear picture of where money is made and lost, not just the company total.

The throughline is that a commercial cleaning company stops judging itself on totals that hide where profit comes from and starts seeing cost to serve and labor at the contract level where profitability is actually decided. The accounts that quietly lose money become visible, the labor overruns become clear, and the company prices, staffs, and manages its contracts on evidence. For a labor business whose margin is the gap between the hours it spends and the price it bid, seeing labor against revenue account by account is what turns the scattered scheduling and financial data into the decisions that make the book of contracts actually profitable, not just busy.
