Dead stock is the most polite problem in the warehouse. It does not stock out, it does not trigger an alert, it does not generate an angry call from a customer. It just sits there, on the shelf and on the balance sheet, looking exactly like an asset while it quietly behaves like a cost. That is what makes it dangerous: every other inventory problem announces itself, and this one does not, so it accumulates until someone decides to go looking. Finding it, and separating the genuinely dead from the merely slow, is a straightforward analytics job that most operations simply never get around to.

## An asset that acts like a cost

Inventory is a positive number on the balance sheet, which frames every unit as value held. For stock that is about to sell, that framing is right. For stock that has stopped selling, it is a polite fiction: the unit is tying up cash, consuming warehouse space, and often slowly losing value, none of which the asset line reflects. The [cash locked in that stock](/blog/cash-to-cash-cycle-importers) could be funding goods that actually turn. Seeing dead stock as the cost it is, rather than the asset it appears to be, is the mental shift the analysis is built to support.

## It whispers while stockouts shout

The reason dead stock accumulates is an asymmetry of attention. A stockout is loud: a shelf is empty, a sale is lost, someone complains, and the problem gets fixed fast. Dead stock is silent: the product is present, the number is positive, and nothing about it demands a response. So operations pour attention into the loud problem and let the quiet one grow, which is backwards in cash terms, because the quiet problem can be larger. Correcting this does not require more vigilance from people; it requires analytics that surface the silent problem deliberately, the same way [exception management](/blog/exception-management-trade) surfaces anything else that is off.

## Aging tells dead from slow

The tool that makes the distinction is aging: grouping inventory by how long it has sat without moving. A slow mover still sells, just too slowly, carrying more [days of supply](/blog/days-of-supply-stockout-risk) than it should and tying up cash longer than needed. Dead stock has effectively stopped and is unlikely to clear at its current price in a reasonable time. The responses differ, so the distinction matters: slow movers often just need a buying adjustment so you stop over-ordering them, while dead stock needs a commercial decision. Aging the inventory, by SKU and by how long each unit has been idle, is what lets you tell which is which instead of lumping all excess together.

## Sharper for importers

For an importer, dead stock stings more, because the cash in it was committed far upstream. You paid for the goods, paid [freight and duty](/blog/landed-cost-calculation) to land them, and funded a long cycle to hold them, so each dead imported unit carries more sunk cost than a dead domestic one, and it cannot be quickly returned or replaced. That raises the value of catching a slow mover early: the sooner it is flagged, the fewer months of committed cash are wasted, and the sooner buying can be corrected so the mistake is not repeated on the next order. Early detection is worth more the longer and more expensive the supply line.

## Finding is analytics; deciding is commercial

What analytics does is find the stock, age it, and quantify what it is costing. What it cannot do is decide what to do with it, because that is a commercial judgment, discount to move it, liquidate it, bundle it, or write it off, that depends on the product and the market. The analysis informs that decision with facts instead of leaving it to be made on a hunch or not made at all. Done in [your own tenant](/blog/analytics-in-your-own-tenant) and refreshed continuously, dead-stock analysis turns an invisible, growing cost into a visible, shrinking list of SKUs with decisions attached.
