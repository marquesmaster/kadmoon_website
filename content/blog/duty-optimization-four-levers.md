When duty rose through 2025 and 2026, "how do we pay less duty" became a board question. The usual answer, a single tactic someone read about, is the wrong shape. Duty optimization is not one move; it is four distinct levers, each targeting a different part of the duty, and the one that pays off depends entirely on your flow. Pulling the wrong lever wastes effort on savings that are not there. The right starting point is not a tactic at all; it is seeing where your duty actually sits.

## Lever one: preference

The first lever is claiming free-trade-agreement treatment on goods that qualify. Under [USMCA](/blog/usmca-2026-review-importers) and other agreements, originating goods can enter duty-free, but only if you certify them against the rules of origin and actually claim the preference. Many operations leave money here, either by not claiming on eligible value or by not knowing which goods would qualify with a small sourcing change. The lever is measured by the FTA qualification rate: eligible value claimed versus left on the table.

## Lever two: deferral and elimination

The second lever is timing and structure. A [Foreign-Trade Zone or bonded warehouse](/blog/foreign-trade-zone-duty-deferral) defers duty until goods enter commerce, eliminates it on re-exports, and can allow an inverted-tariff benefit where the finished-good rate is lower than the components. As duty rises, the cash-flow value of deferral and the savings on re-exports grow with it. This lever pays off for operations with large or slow-moving inventory, significant re-exports, or manufacturing from imported inputs.

## Lever three: recovery

The third lever reclaims duty already paid. [Duty drawback](/blog/duty-drawback-recovering-duty) refunds up to 99% of duty on imported goods that are later exported or destroyed. For an operation that both imports and exports, it can be a meaningful recovery, and it grows with the duty rate. The catch is that not all special tariffs are drawback-eligible, so the recoverable amount has to be calculated carefully rather than assumed. The lever is sized by matching import duty to subsequent exports.

## Lever four: accuracy

The fourth lever is not a program; it is getting the basics right. Wrong [HTS classification](/blog/hts-classification-accuracy) overpays or underpays duty, and a mis-built [customs value](/blog/customs-value-valuation) moves the base the rate applies to. Accuracy is the lever that saves money without any special structure, just by ensuring you are not paying duty on a wrong code or an inflated value. It is also the one that reduces risk at the same time, because the same errors that overpay can underpay and create liability.

## Knowing which lever applies is a data question

The reason generic duty advice fails is that the levers are flow-specific. The only way to know which to pull is to see duty broken down by product, origin, and tariff type: where it concentrates is where a lever can bite. High unclaimed FTA-eligible value points to preference; large duty on long-held or re-exported goods points to a zone or drawback; overpayment from inconsistent codes points to accuracy.
