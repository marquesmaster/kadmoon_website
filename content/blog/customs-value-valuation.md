Most importers read duty off the invoice total times a rate. That is not how it works. Duty is assessed on [customs value](/docs/glossary), a constructed figure that starts from the price paid and adds specific dutiable items, and it is rarely identical to the invoice. Because customs value is both the base for duty and the denominator of every duty ratio, getting it wrong moves the money twice: the duty you pay, and the [effective duty rate](/blog/effective-duty-rate) you use to manage it.

## Customs value is not the invoice

The primary valuation method is transaction value: the price actually paid or payable for the goods sold for export to the United States, plus statutory additions. The invoice is where it starts, not where it ends. Treating the invoice total as the dutiable value skips the additions that belong in it and the charges that do not, and both omissions change the duty. Valuation is a small calculation done on every entry, and like any repeated calculation, it drifts without a consistent method.

## What is dutiable, and what is not

The additions that belong in customs value when they are not already in the price include assists (tooling, materials, or design the buyer provides), royalties and license fees tied to the goods, packing, and certain commissions. On the other side, some charges are treated differently and may not be dutiable when they are separately and correctly identified. The line between dutiable and non-dutiable is exactly where valuation goes right or wrong, and it is specific enough that it needs a rule, not a guess, applied the same way every time.

## The Incoterm decides what is already in the price

Valuation does not stand alone; it depends on the terms of sale. The [Incoterm](/blog/incoterms-and-landed-cost) determines which costs are already inside the seller's price and which the buyer adds, which in turn affects what has to be added to or removed from the invoice to reach customs value. A shipment priced CIF carries freight and insurance in the invoice; one priced FOB does not. Deriving customs value without accounting for the term is a reliable way to double-count or miss a component.

## Why errors stay invisible

A valuation error does not announce itself. Overstated value overpays duty quietly on every affected entry; understated value underpays and builds a liability that surfaces at [liquidation](/docs/glossary) or audit. Because the error is in how the value was built, not in an obviously wrong number, it looks correct on the entry and on the report. The only way to catch it is to compare: is the same supplier's value built the same way across entries, and do the additions appear where they should.

## Checking it is an analytics job

The data to validate customs value is in the import records: invoice values, the additions, the terms, and the duty paid. Comparing how value is constructed across suppliers and entries, flagging the inconsistencies, and tying them to their duty impact turns valuation from an assumption into a checked number. It is the same [customs analytics](/platform) that models duty and [landed cost](/blog/landed-cost-calculation), applied to the figure underneath them. For importers with many suppliers and terms, it is a common place both overpayment and exposure hide.
