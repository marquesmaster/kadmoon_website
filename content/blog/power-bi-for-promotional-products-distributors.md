A promotional products distributor or signage company sells custom orders it sources from suppliers: a customer wants branded merchandise or a sign, the company sources and delivers it, and makes its margin on the gap between what it charges and what the supplier costs. Its profitability depends on real margin per order, supplier performance, and repeat business, and seeing all of that clearly is hard, because order, supplier, and financial data live in separate systems. Power BI's role is to join those systems into one governed view, so order margin, supplier performance, and repeat business are visible where the business is actually decided. This is how these companies use it.

## The business is sourced, and the data is scattered

Ask one of these companies the questions that decide its results, which orders actually make money, which suppliers serve us best, and which customers reorder, and the answer takes someone pulling from order, supplier, and financial systems and assembling it by hand. Order management holds the sales, supplier and purchasing data holds the costs, finance holds the money, and none of it joins into a view of real margin and supplier performance. The company sees pieces, and the picture that matters, true order profitability, supplier performance, repeat business, is reconstructed manually and late.

The first and most valuable thing Power BI does is build a **governed model that joins these systems**, so order margin, supplier performance, and customer repeat business can be read together over time. Power BI reads from the order, supplier, and financial sources, and the model relates them into one coherent picture. Once that model exists, the questions about margin, suppliers, and repeat business become reports the company opens. That join is the foundation, because the business sources custom orders from suppliers, so its profitability depends on connecting what was sold to what it cost, and no single system shows both.

## Order margin: where profitability hides

Because the products are **custom and sourced from different suppliers** at different costs, **order margin varies widely** by product and supplier, and it is not obvious from the order total alone. The same selling price can carry very different margins depending on what the item cost from the supplier, so some orders earn well while others barely cover their cost once supplier pricing and the work involved are counted. A company watching order totals or revenue without seeing real margin can be busy and still not know which of its business is actually profitable.

A governed model that shows **real margin per order**, selling price against supplier cost, lets a company see which products, suppliers, and orders are actually profitable. With that visible, it can price and source deliberately, steering toward the products and suppliers that earn well and addressing the ones that do not, rather than assuming the order total reflects the profit. For a business where margin is the gap between what it charges and what it sources at, and that gap varies enormously order to order, seeing real order margin is the core of managing profitability, because the profit lives in that gap and the order total hides it. A company that can see margin per order prices and sources on evidence; one that cannot is guessing at which of its own business makes money.

## Supplier performance: margin and experience

The company's suppliers drive both its **margin** and its **customer experience**, so **supplier performance**, in **cost** and in **reliability**, is central, and the governed view makes it visible. Suppliers vary in what they **charge**, which drives margin, and in how **reliably** they deliver on time and to spec, which drives whether the customer gets what they ordered when promised. Both matter, and a company that cannot see supplier performance clearly cannot manage either well.

A governed model that shows **supplier performance** lets a company see which suppliers are most **cost-effective** and most **reliable**, so it can direct business to the best ones and manage the rest. With that visible, it can protect **margin** by sourcing from cost-effective suppliers and protect the **customer experience** by relying on suppliers that deliver, while addressing or avoiding those that do not. This matters because in a sourced business, the supplier is effectively part of the company's delivery to the customer, so a supplier that is unreliable or expensive hurts the company directly, in margin and in the customer relationship. Seeing supplier performance clearly is how the company manages the partners its own results depend on, which is a large part of running a sourcing business well.

## Repeat business: building on the customers you have

Much of a promotional products or signage company's revenue comes from customers **reordering**, so **repeat and reorder business** is a major driver that is easy to miss without a clear view of customers **over time.** A customer who reorders regularly, the same branded merchandise, new signage, seasonal reorders, is worth far more than a one-time order, and nurturing repeat business is often the most **efficient growth** available, but it requires seeing which customers reorder and which have lapsed, which is hard when the view is order by order rather than customer over time.

A governed model that shows **customer activity over time** reveals the repeat business. The company can see which customers reorder and how often, identify its valuable **repeat customers** and its **lapsed** ones worth re-engaging, and grow the reorder revenue that is already within reach. For a business where a loyal reordering customer is a significant asset, seeing repeat business is how the company **builds on the customers it has** rather than constantly chasing new ones, which is both more efficient and more durable. The reorder revenue is often the most profitable and most overlooked, because it is easy to focus on new orders and miss the pattern of who keeps coming back, and the governed view is what makes that pattern visible enough to act on.

## It fits companies of any size

A smaller company benefits as clearly as a large one, because even a modest operation runs orders, suppliers, and financials across separate systems reconciled by hand, and real margin, supplier performance, and repeat business matter just as much. Bringing it into one governed view gives a smaller company a clear picture without a data team, through a partner who builds it and a managed arrangement that keeps it running. Larger companies run more orders and suppliers, but the core value, seeing order margin, supplier performance, and repeat business, applies at any size, and the underlying economics are the same whether the company is small or large.

## What these companies end up with

A promotional products or signage company running on Power BI ends up with:

- Order, supplier, and financial data joined so real margin and supplier performance can be read together.
- Real margin per order visible, so which products, suppliers, and orders pay becomes clear.
- Supplier performance in cost and reliability visible, so margin and customer experience are protected.
- Repeat business seen over time, so reorder revenue and lapsed customers become visible.

The throughline is that a promotional products or signage company stops piecing its margin and supplier picture together by hand and starts seeing real order margin, supplier performance, and repeat business on one governed view, where the business is actually decided. The margin hidden in the gap between selling price and supplier cost becomes visible, the suppliers the company depends on get managed on cost and reliability, and the repeat business that drives so much revenue becomes something the company can see and grow. For a business that sources custom orders and lives on margin and reorders, seeing all of that clearly is what turns the scattered order and supplier data into the decisions that protect margin and build on the customers the company already has.
