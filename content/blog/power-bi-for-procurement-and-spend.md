Procurement exists to get the organization what it needs at the best value, and it cannot do that without seeing what the organization actually spends. Yet most organizations genuinely cannot see their own spend clearly, because it is scattered across systems and categorized inconsistently, so the same supplier looks like several and the real totals are hidden. Power BI's role for a procurement team is to join the purchasing, supplier, and financial data into one governed view, so total spend becomes visible by supplier and category, and the savings and risks hidden in the scattered data come to light. This is how procurement teams use it.

## You cannot see your own spend

Ask an organization a question that should be simple, how much do we spend with this supplier in total, and across how many of them do we buy this category, and most genuinely cannot answer. The reason is that spend is **scattered across systems** and **categorized inconsistently.** Purchases happen in different systems and departments. Suppliers are named inconsistently, so one vendor appears as several. Categories do not line up across sources. The result is that the true picture of where the money goes is fragmented beyond recognition.

This is a real and surprisingly universal problem: organizations spend large sums without being able to see clearly what they spend with whom or on what, because nobody has joined and consistently classified the data. The spend is happening, but the visibility is not there.

The first and most valuable thing Power BI does is build a **governed model that joins the sources and applies consistent categorization**, so total spend can be read by category, supplier, department, and over time. Power BI reads from the ERP, purchasing, accounts payable, and supplier systems, and the model relates and classifies them into one coherent, consistently categorized view. Once that model exists, the question that had no answer, what do we really spend, and with whom, becomes a report you open. This visibility, which most organizations have never actually had, is the foundation of everything procurement can do with its spend.

## What spend visibility reveals

Once spend is visible in one consistent view, it routinely reveals **savings and risks that were hidden** in the scattered data. These are the opportunities procurement exists to capture, and they are invisible until the data is brought together.

**Duplicate and fragmented suppliers.** The same thing bought from many vendors at many prices, where consolidating would command better terms. Fragmented buying is a common and expensive pattern, and it is invisible when the buying is spread across systems.

**Maverick spend.** Purchases made outside preferred suppliers and negotiated contracts, which cost more and undermine the deals procurement worked to secure. You cannot reduce maverick spend you cannot find, and the governed view finds it.

**Supplier concentration.** Where too much depends on a single vendor, which is a risk to manage. Concentration is hard to see when spend is fragmented, and clear once it is joined.

**Category patterns.** Where spending in a category points at an opportunity to negotiate or consolidate, visible only when the category is consistently classified across all the sources.

These are the classic findings of spend analysis, and they share a cause: they are hidden by scattered, inconsistently categorized data, and revealed by a governed model that brings the spend together. For a procurement team, surfacing these is where the savings opportunities come from.

## Visibility is the foundation of savings

It is worth being precise about how spend visibility leads to savings, because the visibility does not save money by itself; it is the **precondition** for every saving procurement can drive.

Consider the procurement levers. You cannot negotiate a better deal with a supplier if you do not know your total spend with them, and spend visibility is what gives you that number. You cannot consolidate fragmented buying if you cannot see it is fragmented, and the governed view is what shows the fragmentation. You cannot enforce preferred suppliers and reduce maverick spend if you cannot find the off-contract purchases, and the visibility is what surfaces them. Each procurement saving depends on first seeing the real picture.

So spend visibility is the foundation that makes all of these levers usable. Once total spend is clear by supplier and category, procurement can consolidate, negotiate from a position of knowledge rather than guesswork, and enforce its preferred suppliers, each of which saves money. The principle, "you cannot manage what you cannot see," is especially literal in procurement, where the inability to see spend directly prevents the actions that would reduce it. Making spend visible is the first and indispensable step toward every procurement saving, which is why it is where the value starts.

## What procurement teams end up with

A procurement team running on Power BI ends up with:

- Purchasing, supplier, and financial data joined and consistently categorized, so total spend is visible by supplier, category, and department.
- Hidden savings surfaced, duplicate suppliers, fragmented buying, maverick spend, and concentration risk.
- One clear view of where the money actually goes, which most organizations have never had.
- The visibility that makes procurement's savings levers, consolidation, negotiation, supplier enforcement, usable.

The throughline is that a procurement team stops operating without being able to see its own spend and starts working from one clear, consistently categorized view of where the money goes. The hidden fragmentation, the maverick spend, the concentration risk, and the consolidation opportunities all become visible, and with them the actions that capture value. For a function whose entire purpose is getting value from the organization's spending, finally being able to see that spending clearly is what turns the purchasing data the organization already generates into the savings and risk decisions procurement exists to make.
