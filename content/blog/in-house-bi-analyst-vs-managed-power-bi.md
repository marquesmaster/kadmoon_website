When Power BI becomes important enough to invest in, the question is usually framed as "should we hire someone?" It is the wrong first question. The right one is "what does the work actually require, and how steady is it?" Because the honest comparison is not one analyst versus nothing; it is one analyst versus a team you can rent. This guide lays out the trade-offs so you can make the call on evidence rather than instinct.

## What the work actually requires

Good BI is not one job. It is data modeling and DAX, the [data engineering](/services/data-engineering) that feeds a clean model, and the [governance](/services/data-governance) that keeps it trusted and secure. A single "BI analyst" is usually strong in one or two of those and thin in the rest, so whichever corner they are weakest in becomes the corner your platform is weakest in. That is not a knock on any individual; it is just that the role, done well, spans more than one person's depth.

## The true cost of a hire

A senior Power BI hire in the US costs well over one hundred thousand dollars a year once you load salary with benefits, payroll, tooling, and overhead. That figure understates the real cost in two ways. Recruiting takes months and often a fee, and even a strong hire needs time to ramp into your data, your stakeholders, and your priorities before they are fully productive. So the first several months of that salary buy ramp, not output. None of that is wasted if you genuinely need a full-time owner, but it is the real number to compare against, not the base salary alone.

## What a managed team gives you

A [managed Power BI team](/packages) is the same skill set, rented. Instead of one person you get modeling, data engineering, and governance under one agreement, with a response SLA, for a fixed monthly fee that is usually less than one loaded hire. Three properties matter most:

- **Coverage.** The specialist skills are already on the team, so there is no weak corner and no single point of failure.
- **Elasticity.** You buy capacity, so you can scale up for a big build and back down afterward instead of carrying a fixed headcount through the quiet months.
- **No overhead.** No recruiting, no management, no turnover risk. The people who scope the work are the ones who do it.

## When to hire anyway

Buying a team is not always the answer. Hire in-house when you need someone full-time and deeply embedded, when there is enough steady work to keep a dedicated person genuinely busy, and when constant proximity to stakeholders outweighs breadth of skills. A great in-house owner who lives inside your business context is valuable and hard to replicate.

## The hybrid most teams land on

In practice the strongest setup is often both: a lean in-house owner who holds context and priorities, plus a managed team that supplies capacity, specialist skills, and governance the owner's calendar cannot absorb. The owner decides what matters; the team delivers the volume and the depth. You get proximity and coverage without betting your entire reporting function on one person's availability.
