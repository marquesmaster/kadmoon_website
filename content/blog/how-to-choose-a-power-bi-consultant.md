There is no shortage of people who can build a good-looking Power BI dashboard. There is a real shortage of consultants who build one on a model that reconciles, stays fast, is governed, and that your team can own after they leave. Choosing well is mostly about knowing what to look at instead of the demo, and what to ask before you sign. This guide gives you both.

## What a good Power BI consultant actually does

The dashboard is the visible ten percent. The value is underneath it. A strong [Power BI consultant](/services/power-bi) designs a governed semantic model with [one definition per KPI](https://learn.microsoft.com/power-bi/guidance/star-schema), builds the pipelines that feed clean data into it, implements [row-level security](https://learn.microsoft.com/power-bi/enterprise/service-admin-rls) and governance, and only then builds reports, each tied to a decision someone actually makes. Judge a consultant on that foundation, because a pretty report on a broken model is the thing everyone stops trusting three months in.

## Ask how they scope and price

The single most predictive question is how work is priced. An open-ended hourly arrangement puts the risk on you and rewards the meter running long. The healthier pattern is a short, fixed-scope [assessment](/services) that produces a costed plan, then a fixed price for the build, so you know the scope, timeline, and cost before delivery starts. You are buying an outcome, not hours.

## Insist on your tenant and your ownership

Everything should be built and kept in your own Microsoft tenant: workspaces, models, gateways, reports. Paired with documentation and enablement, that ownership means you are never trapped, you can continue in-house or change providers whenever you want. A consultant who keeps the work in their environment, or is vague about handover, is selling you dependency. That is lock-in, and it is a reason to walk.

## Check who actually does the work

Ask directly: will the senior person in this meeting be the one building my model, or does delivery hand off to juniors after the contract is signed? Bait-and-switch staffing is common and costly. The people who [scope your project](/about) should be the ones who build it, because the context does not transfer cleanly and quality follows seniority. Juniors on your budget is where both leak away.

## Make governance a requirement, not a hope

Governance is not a phase you get to later. Row-level security, [Microsoft Purview](https://learn.microsoft.com/purview/) lineage, and data loss prevention should ship with the build, and there should be one agreed definition of each KPI so revenue, margin, and churn mean the same thing in every report. Ask how the consultant handles this on day one. If the answer is "we can add that afterward," you are buying a cleanup project you do not know about yet.

## The tell-tale red flags

Five signals that predict a bad engagement:

1. A price quoted before anyone has seen your data.
2. Work kept in the consultant's tenant, or evasiveness about handover.
3. Governance treated as an optional add-on.
4. A senior on the sales call, juniors on the delivery.
5. Demos heavy on visuals, light on the model and security underneath.
