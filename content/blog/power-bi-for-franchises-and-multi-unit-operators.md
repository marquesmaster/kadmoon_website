A franchise or multi-unit operation is, at its heart, a business of comparison: the whole model depends on seeing how units perform relative to each other, spreading what works, and supporting what struggles. Yet comparing units is exactly what most operators cannot do reliably, because each location runs its own system and counts things its own way, so the numbers never agree and the comparison is apples to oranges. Power BI's role for a multi-unit operation is to join every location into one governed view where units are measured the same way and genuinely comparable. This is how franchisors and operators use it.

## Comparison is the whole point, and it does not work

The multi-unit model runs on **comparison.** An operator or franchisor manages the network by seeing which units are doing well and which are not, understanding why, and acting on it, spreading the practices of strong units and supporting weak ones. Comparison across units is not a nice-to-have; it is the core management activity of a multi-unit business.

And it usually does not work, because each unit counts things its own way. One location's sales, another's measured on a slightly different basis, a third's with different categorization, do not line up, so comparing them is comparing apples to oranges. Stacking or averaging inconsistent numbers produces a picture that misleads, and operators often do not realize how much the inconsistency distorts what they are seeing. The comparison the whole model depends on is unreliable, precisely because the units are not measured the same way.

This is the problem Power BI solves. A **governed model that joins the units** defines each metric once, so every location is measured identically and the comparison is genuinely fair. Power BI reads from each unit's systems, and the model brings them into one consistent view where the units line up. That consistency, not prettier reports, is the point, because it is what makes the comparison reliable, and reliable comparison is what a multi-unit operation is managed by.

## Consistent comparison, and what it enables

Once every unit is measured the same way, the operator can finally do the things the multi-unit model is built on. **Rank the units fairly**, seeing the top and bottom performers on a genuine like-for-like basis. **Understand why** the strong units are strong, investigating what they do differently so their practices can be identified and spread. **Spot the units that need support** before their problems become serious, and act. And **see the network as a whole** while being able to drill into any single location.

This is **benchmarking**, and it turns a network of locations from a set of separate businesses you cannot compare into a source of insight about what actually drives performance. When units are comparable, the differences between them become informative: the gap between a strong and a weak unit, read against what each does differently, points at what matters, which practices lift performance, which conditions hurt it, which support helps. The network teaches the operator about its own business, because the units are a natural experiment that only becomes readable when they are measured consistently.

For an operator, this is the whole return. A network of comparable units is a managed, improving network; a network of units that each count things differently is a collection of businesses the operator cannot really compare or learn from. Consistent comparison is what makes the difference, and it is exactly what the governed model provides.

## Franchisor and operator alike

The approach serves both a **franchisor**, overseeing franchisees, and a **multi-unit operator**, owning its locations, with the same pattern. A franchisor needs to see across franchisees, track network performance, and support units, often alongside royalty and compliance reporting that the franchise relationship requires. An operator who owns its units needs to compare and manage them directly. In both cases, the core is identical: join the units into a governed model, measure them consistently, and benchmark and manage from one comparable view.

A franchisor gets the added dimension of the franchisor-franchisee relationship and its reporting, royalties, compliance, support, built on the same governed foundation. But the fundamental need, comparing locations that each count things differently, is the same whether you franchise or own the units, so the same solution serves both. The multi-unit challenge, and its answer, does not depend on the ownership model; it depends on having many units that must be compared, which is exactly what a governed model makes possible.

## What multi-unit operators end up with

A franchise or multi-unit operation running on Power BI ends up with:

- Every unit joined into a governed model and measured the same way, so comparison is genuinely fair.
- Units ranked and benchmarked reliably, so top and bottom performers are seen on a like-for-like basis.
- Insight into what drives performance, from reading the comparable units against what each does differently.
- The whole network managed from one consistent picture, with drill-down to any location.

The throughline is that a multi-unit operation stops trying to compare units that each count things differently and starts managing a network of genuinely comparable locations. The strong units' practices become visible and spreadable, the struggling units get support in time, and the network becomes a source of insight about what works. For a business whose entire model is running many units and learning across them, having those units measured consistently and comparably is what turns a stack of separate location reports into a managed, improving network, which is the whole reason the multi-unit model exists.
