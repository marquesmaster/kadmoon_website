Organizations do not become data-driven in one leap. They progress through recognizable stages, from reporting by hand in spreadsheets to trustworthy analytics on a governed foundation, and the most useful thing you can do is understand where you actually are on that journey. Knowing your stage tells you what the right next step is, which differs depending on where you stand, and it prevents the common and costly mistake of trying to skip ahead. Here is a practical way to understand the data maturity journey and locate yourself on it.

## The progression, in plain terms

Organizations move through a recognizable progression of data maturity. The exact labels vary between frameworks and matter less than the direction, but the stages are clear enough in practice.

Early on, reporting is done **by hand in spreadsheets.** Data is exported, pasted, and assembled manually, the numbers often disagree, and reporting is slow and fragile. This is where many organizations start and some stay longer than they should.

Next comes **reporting tools without governance.** The organization adopts a BI tool and builds reports, which is progress, but without a governed model underneath, the numbers still conflict, because each report defines things its own way. There is capability but not trust, a common and frustrating middle stage.

Then comes **governed, trusted analytics.** A proper semantic model with defined metrics means the numbers reconcile and reporting is trusted. This is the stage where analytics genuinely pays off, because the reporting can actually be relied on for decisions.

Beyond that, the governed foundation enables **more advanced capabilities**, deeper analytics, AI, and the rest, built on the trust and consistency the earlier stages established.

The value of seeing this progression is not in the precise labels but in recognizing the **direction of travel**, from scattered, manual, and inconsistent toward joined-up, automated, and trustworthy, and in locating yourself on it.

## Telling which stage you are at

To find your stage, look honestly at how your reporting actually works, not at how advanced you wish you were. A few signs tell you reliably where you stand.

If you report mostly **by hand in spreadsheets** that disagree, you are early. The manual assembly and the conflicting numbers are the signature of the first stage, whatever tools you may also own.

If you have **reporting tools but numbers still conflict**, with no governed model underneath, you are in the middle stage, with capability but without trust. The sign is that you have dashboards but still cannot fully rely on them, because they disagree with each other.

If you have a **governed model where numbers reconcile** and reporting is trusted, you are at the advanced stage and ready to build further. The sign is that the numbers agree across reports and people act on them without double-checking.

The honest indicators, then, are whether your numbers agree, whether reporting is manual or automated, and whether there is a governed foundation. These tell you where you stand far more reliably than any aspirational self-assessment, because they describe what is actually true of your reporting rather than what you intend.

## The mistake: skipping stages

The biggest and most common mistake organizations make is **trying to skip stages**, reaching for advanced capabilities before the foundation that supports them exists.

The clearest example is wanting **AI or advanced analytics while the data is still scattered and inconsistent.** This does not work, because advanced capabilities depend on the governed foundation that an earlier stage builds, and without it they produce confident wrong answers rather than insight. An organization that tries to jump from messy data straight to AI skips the trust-building step and gets amplified mess, not intelligence.

The mistake is understandable, because the advanced capabilities are the exciting ones and the foundational work is not. But the stages exist for a reason: each depends on the one before. You cannot have trustworthy advanced analytics without trustworthy data, and you cannot have trustworthy data without the governed foundation, which is exactly the work that skipping stages tries to avoid. The excitement of the end state pulls organizations to leap ahead, and the leap fails, because the dependencies were skipped. Recognizing that each stage requires the previous one is what prevents this expensive error.

## The right next step is the next step

The practical payoff of locating your stage is knowing the **right next step**, which is always the one that fixes your current stage's main limitation, not a leap to the end.

If you are reporting **by hand**, the right next step is a **governed model** that makes reporting consistent and automated, which fixes the manual, inconsistent limitation of the spreadsheet stage. Not advanced analytics, the governed foundation.

If you have **tools but no trust**, the right next step is the **governed foundation** that makes the numbers reconcile, which fixes the core limitation of the capability-without-trust stage. Not more tools, the model underneath them.

If you have a **trusted foundation**, the right next step is **building further on it**, more coverage, more advanced capabilities, which is now appropriate because the foundation exists to support it.

In every case, the right move is the next step from where you are, the one that resolves the current stage's limitation and moves you forward, rather than anything further ahead. Trying to do more than the next step tends to fail, because it skips a dependency; taking exactly the next step works, because it builds on what you have toward what you need.

## Progress, not perfection

The goal of understanding the data maturity journey is not to rush to the end or to feel behind for not being there. It is to take the **right next step from wherever you actually are.** An organization reporting by hand is not failing; it is at a stage, and its path forward is clear: a governed model. An organization with tools but no trust is not stuck; it needs the foundation that makes the numbers reconcile. Each has a sensible next step, and taking it moves them forward.

This reframes data maturity as a journey everyone is somewhere on, rather than a standard to be judged against. Wherever you are, there is a right next step, and progress comes from taking it, then the one after that, rather than from leaping toward an end state the foundation cannot yet support. The organizations that mature well are not the ones that reach furthest fastest; they are the ones that correctly identify their stage and take the right next step from it, repeatedly, building the foundation before the capabilities that need it. Locate yourself honestly, take the next step, and the journey takes care of itself, one well-founded step at a time.
