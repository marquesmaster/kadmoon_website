A coworking or flexible office operator runs on a recurring relationship with its members: it fills space with memberships that pay month after month, and its economics depend on how full its locations are, what that occupancy is worth, and how well it retains the members who make up the base. Seeing all of that clearly is hard, because membership, access, and financial data live in separate systems and usually across multiple locations, so the operator tends to see each system and location in isolation. Power BI's role is to join those systems into one governed view, so occupancy, recurring revenue, and retention are visible across every location. This is how coworking operators use it.

## The picture is split across systems and locations

Ask a coworking operator a question that should span the business, how full are our locations, how is recurring revenue trending, and who is churning, and the answer takes someone pulling from the membership system, checking occupancy, and assembling financials location by location by hand. Membership software holds the members and plans, access and space systems hold occupancy, finance holds the revenue, and none of it joins into a view of the recurring economics across locations. The operator sees pieces, per location, and the whole picture of how full, how profitable, and how stable the business is gets reconstructed manually and late.

The first and most valuable thing Power BI does is build a **governed model that joins these systems**, so occupancy, recurring revenue, membership mix, and retention can be read together by location, membership type, and over time. Power BI reads from membership, access, and financial sources across the locations, and the model relates them into one coherent picture. Once that model exists, the business-wide questions that took manual assembly become reports the operator opens. That join is the foundation, because the operator runs a member base across locations for recurring revenue, and no single system or location shows that whole.

## Occupancy and recurring revenue together

The core of coworking economics is **occupancy** and **recurring revenue** seen **together**, because a location needs to be not just full but **profitably full.** Occupancy is the use of the space; recurring revenue is what that use is worth, and the two are not the same, because different membership types, a hot desk, a dedicated desk, a private office, pay very differently. A location can be highly occupied at low-value memberships, or less full at higher-value ones, and occupancy alone cannot tell which, so it cannot tell how the location is actually doing economically.

A governed model that shows **occupancy alongside recurring revenue by membership type** reveals the real picture. The operator can see how full each location is, what that occupancy is **worth**, and where the opportunity lies, whether to grow occupancy where there is space, or grow its **value** by shifting the membership mix toward higher-value plans. This turns a simple occupancy number into an understanding of the location's **actual economics**, which is what the operator needs to manage it, because two locations at the same occupancy can have very different revenue, and only seeing both together shows which is really performing and where the room to improve is. For a business where the same space can be filled in more or less valuable ways, seeing occupancy and its value together is the core of running it well.

## Member retention: the engine of recurring revenue

Because coworking is a **recurring-revenue business**, **member retention** is the engine, and churn quietly undermines growth if it is not watched. Membership revenue recurs, which makes the **member base the core asset**, and a member who leaves is recurring revenue lost that new sign-ups have to **replace before the business grows.** Churn is a leak, and signing new members faster does not help if the base is leaking as fast as it fills. Yet retention is often **less visible** than new sales, because members leave quietly, one at a time, and the aggregate churn rate is not something the membership system puts in front of anyone.

A governed model that **surfaces churn**, who is leaving, at what rate, and any patterns behind it, makes retention manageable. The operator can see its churn rate and trend, which membership types or locations lose members fastest, and any patterns worth addressing, so it can work retention deliberately, protecting the member base rather than only chasing new members to cover losses it cannot clearly see. For a recurring-revenue business whose value is its member base, **managing churn is as central as filling the space**, and the governed view is what makes the quiet, cumulative problem of churn visible enough to manage. A location that signs many new members but loses them quietly is on a treadmill; one that retains well compounds its base, and only seeing retention clearly tells the operator which it has.

## It fits operators of any size

A single-location operator benefits as clearly as a multi-site one, because even one space runs membership, occupancy, and financial data across separate systems reconciled by hand, and occupancy, recurring revenue, and churn matter just as much. Bringing it into one governed view gives a single location a clear picture of its economics without a data team, through a partner who builds it and a managed arrangement that keeps it running. Multi-site operators gain the ability to compare locations and spot which perform best, but the core value, seeing occupancy, recurring revenue, and retention clearly, applies to a single space too, because the underlying economics are the same at any size.

## What coworking operators end up with

A coworking or flexible office operator running on Power BI ends up with:

- Membership, access, and financial data joined so the recurring economics can be read across locations.
- Occupancy and recurring revenue seen together, so profitably-full is distinguished from merely full.
- Member churn surfaced, so retention is managed as deliberately as new sign-ups.
- A clear view of the member base and the space without the manual, location-by-location assembly.

The throughline is that a coworking operator stops seeing membership, occupancy, and finance in separate systems and locations and starts managing occupancy, recurring revenue, and retention on one governed view across the business. Occupancy gains its economic meaning when seen against the revenue it produces, churn becomes visible enough to manage, and the member base is grown deliberately rather than churned away. For a recurring-revenue business that fills space with members, seeing how full, how profitable, and how stable each location is is what turns the scattered membership and financial data into the decisions that keep the spaces full, the revenue growing, and the member base intact.
