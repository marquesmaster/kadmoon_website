A furniture or home goods retailer has an inventory problem that most retailers do not: its stock is **expensive and slow-moving.** A sofa or dining set costs a lot to hold and may sit for months, so capital tied up in the wrong items is a real and ongoing cost. On top of that, much of what it sells is **special-ordered** and must flow through to **delivery**, which is where the customer experience is often won or lost. Seeing all of this clearly is hard, because sales, inventory, and order data live in separate systems. Power BI's role is to join them into one governed view, so turns, special orders, and delivery are visible. This is how furniture retailers use it.

## Expensive inventory, scattered data

Ask a furniture retailer the questions that decide its results, what is actually turning, what is tying up our capital, and how are our orders flowing to delivery, and the answer takes someone pulling from sales, inventory, and order systems and assembling it by hand. The POS holds sales, inventory holds what is in stock, special-order and purchase systems hold what is on the way, and none of it joins into a view of the expensive inventory and the order flow. The retailer sees pieces, and the picture that matters, where its capital is working and where it is stuck, and how orders are reaching customers, is reconstructed manually and late.

The first and most valuable thing Power BI does is build a **governed model that joins these systems**, so inventory turns, special-order flow, and delivery can be read together by product, category, and over time. Power BI reads from the sales, inventory, and order sources, and the model relates them into one coherent picture. Once that model exists, the questions about turns and the order-to-delivery flow become reports the retailer opens. That join is the foundation, because managing a furniture business depends on seeing expensive inventory and the special-order pipeline together, and no single system shows both.

## Inventory turns: managing expensive, slow stock

For a furniture retailer, **inventory turns** matter intensely, because the stock is **expensive and slow**, so every slow-moving item is **real money locked up** and floor space consumed. Unlike fast-moving retail, where turns are quick and a slow item is a minor drag, a furniture retailer holds high-value inventory that can sit for a long time, so the capital tied up in the wrong items is a significant cost that compounds the longer it sits. Managing which inventory to hold is, in large part, managing the business's capital.

A governed model that shows **turns by product and category** lets a retailer see what is actually moving and what is sitting. With that visible, it can buy more of what **turns**, clear what does **not**, and free the capital that slow stock ties up, redirecting it toward inventory that sells. This is central to furniture retail economics, because the inventory is the largest use of capital and the slowest to convert, so seeing turns clearly is seeing where the capital is working and where it is dead. A retailer that can see its turns manages its inventory as the capital investment it is; one that cannot carries slow stock it never quite notices is costing it, because the cost of expensive inventory sitting is invisible until the turns are laid out plainly.

## Special orders and delivery: the customer experience

Much of what a furniture retailer sells is **special-ordered**, taking time to arrive and then be delivered, so the **order-to-delivery flow** is where a lot of the **customer experience** is won or lost, and it is exactly the part that is hard to see when the data is scattered. A customer who buys a special-order sofa is waiting, sometimes for weeks, and their experience depends on that order flowing smoothly and the retailer being able to tell them where it is. When the order data sits across systems, the retailer often cannot easily answer "where is my order?", which is a frequent source of frustration.

A governed model that tracks **orders from placement through arrival to delivery** makes the pipeline visible. The retailer can see where orders are, where delays are happening, and how delivery is performing, so it can **manage the flow** and keep customers informed rather than chasing status across systems. Seeing the pipeline clearly turns a common source of customer frustration into something the retailer can actually **manage**: it can spot delays early, address the bottlenecks that recur, and give customers accurate information, all of which directly affect satisfaction on a high-value purchase people care a lot about. For a business where the sale is big and the wait is real, managing the order-to-delivery flow well is a meaningful part of the experience, and it depends on seeing that flow, which the governed view provides and scattered systems hide.

## It fits retailers of any size

A smaller furniture retailer benefits as clearly as a large chain, because even a single store holds expensive, slow-moving inventory and runs special orders and deliveries whose data lives in separate systems reconciled by hand. Bringing it into one governed view gives a smaller retailer a clear picture of turns, orders, and delivery without a data team, through a partner who builds it and a managed arrangement that keeps it running. Larger chains run more stores and SKUs, but the core value, seeing turns and the order-to-delivery flow on expensive inventory, applies at any size, and the underlying challenge is the same whether you run one store or many.

## What furniture retailers end up with

A furniture or home goods retailer running on Power BI ends up with:

- Sales, inventory, and order data joined so turns and the order flow can be read together.
- Inventory turns visible by product and category, so capital tied up in slow stock becomes clear.
- The special-order-to-delivery pipeline visible, so the customer experience can be managed.
- A clear view of expensive inventory and the order flow without the manual assembly or a data team.

The throughline is that a furniture retailer stops managing expensive, slow inventory and a special-order pipeline from scattered systems and starts seeing turns and the order-to-delivery flow on one governed view. The capital tied up in slow stock becomes visible and manageable, the order flow that shapes the customer experience becomes something the retailer can see and run, and the business is managed on evidence. For a retailer whose inventory is costly and slow and whose sales often come with a wait, seeing turns and the order flow clearly is what turns the scattered sales and order data into the decisions that free up capital and keep customers happy through the delivery of what they bought.
