Of all the inputs to landed cost, freight moves the most. It is usually the largest volatile component, and unlike duty, which follows a rate, freight is a shifting mix of base cost, fuel, and surcharges that changes by carrier, lane, and week. That volatility is exactly why it is worth analyzing, and exactly why it resists analysis: a pile of freight invoices does not tell you where the cost is coming from or whether you are being billed fairly.

## Freight is the big moving part

Duty is large but rule-bound; freight is large and loose. Because it is often the biggest variable line in [landed cost](/blog/landed-cost-calculation), a swing in freight moves margin more than most other components, and it swings for reasons that are not always visible, fuel, capacity, peak season, a new accessorial. An operation that watches duty closely but treats freight as a lump sum is managing the smaller, steadier number and ignoring the larger, moving one.

## The invoice hides the cost

A freight invoice is not a price; it is a bundle. Base rate, fuel surcharge, and a changing set of accessorials are rolled together, and the mix differs by carrier and shipment. That bundling is why raw freight spend is so hard to compare: when a lane gets more expensive, the total does not tell you whether the base rate rose, fuel spiked, or an accessorial crept in. Decomposing the invoice into its components is the first step, because you cannot manage what the invoice has blended into a single figure.

## Normalize to compare

Once invoices are decomposed, the next step is normalization: expressing freight as cost per lane and mode, with base, fuel, and accessorials separated, measured the same way across carriers. That is what makes comparison honest. Normalized, you can see that one carrier is actually cheaper on a lane once surcharges are counted, that a lane's increase came from an accessorial rather than the rate, or that two similar lanes are priced differently. [Freight cost per lane](/docs/kpi-library) is only meaningful once it is normalized this way.

## Surcharge creep and billing errors

Two things hide in undecomposed freight: surcharge creep and billing errors. Accessorials have a way of accumulating, a charge added here, a fee that was supposed to be temporary staying on, and because they are buried in the total, they persist unnoticed. Billing errors, duplicate charges, wrong rates, mis-applied surcharges, are common in freight and are only catchable when invoices are checked against what was agreed. Both are money recovered simply by looking, which requires the decomposed, normalized view.

## Allocate it to the unit

Freight analysis is not complete until it connects to the unit. Allocating freight to each product by its share of a shipment turns a lump sum into a per-unit cost, which is what feeds [landed cost](/blog/landed-cost-calculation), pricing, and [sourcing](/blog/supplier-analytics-landed-cost). Tracked only as total spend, freight leaves landed cost approximate; decomposed, normalized, and allocated, it makes landed cost and margin accurate.
