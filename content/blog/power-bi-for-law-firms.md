A law firm is a business that sells time, and its profitability turns on a few numbers that are notoriously hard to see clearly: how much of each timekeeper's capacity is billable, how much of the time recorded actually turns into cash, and whether individual matters make money. The data behind these, time entries, billing, collections, matter details, lives in separate systems that do not reconcile, so the numbers get assembled by hand, late, and inconsistently. Power BI's role for a law firm is to join those systems into one governed view, so the economics that run the firm are visible and trusted. This is how firms use it.

## The numbers that run a firm, in separate systems

Ask a law firm a question that should be simple, what is our true realization this quarter by practice, and watch how hard it is to answer confidently. Time entries live in the time system. Billings and collections live in the billing system. Matter and client details live in practice management. Each holds its own piece, none reconciles with the others, and the real economics of a timekeeper, a matter, or a client has to be pieced together by hand.

The first and most valuable thing Power BI does is build a **governed model that joins these systems**, so hours, rates, billings, write-downs, and collections can be read together by timekeeper, matter, practice, and client. Power BI reads from the time, billing, and practice management systems, and the model relates them into one coherent picture. Once that model exists, the questions that used to take days of assembly become reports you open. That join is the foundation, because every number that matters to a firm spans the systems, and no single one holds the whole answer.

## Realization: the money already earned

The number where law firms most quietly lose money is **realization**, the chain from time recorded to time billed to cash collected, and the gaps at each step. Time gets written down before it is billed. Bills get discounted at collection. Each gap is revenue the firm has already done the work to earn and then given away, and because the stages live in different systems, the leakage is hard to see.

A governed model brings the whole chain together, so realization is visible by timekeeper, matter, practice, and client, and the firm can see where the leakage concentrates. That turns realization from a figure reported after the fact into something **diagnosable and manageable**: you can see which timekeepers, matters, or clients drive the write-downs, and address the causes, scope creep that is not billed, rates that are not holding, collection practices that discount too readily. Because every avoided write-down is revenue the firm has already earned, the return on improving realization is direct. For most firms, this is where the analytics most clearly pays for itself, by protecting money that is currently leaking out unseen.

## Matter profitability, while you can still act

The other high-value capability is seeing **matter profitability in flight**. Matter economics depend on the time and cost against what is actually billed and collected, and when that is only assembled at the end, a losing matter is a post-mortem: you learn it lost money after it is too late to do anything.

A governed model brings the pieces together so matter economics are visible while the matter is still running. A firm can see an over-serviced or under-billed matter early enough to manage it, adjusting staffing, addressing scope, or having the client conversation while it still changes the outcome. Profitability seen only at write-down explains what happened; profitability seen during the matter lets you shape it. For a firm whose margin is made one matter at a time, moving that visibility from after to during is exactly where the analytics earns its keep, because it converts hindsight into a decision you can still act on.

## Utilization, consistent and current

The third core number is **utilization**, how much of each timekeeper's capacity is billable, and like the others it only helps if it is consistent and current. A governed model defines utilization once and makes it visible by timekeeper, team, and practice, comparable across the firm rather than computed differently in each report. And because it refreshes on a schedule, utilization is visible as it develops rather than reconstructed after the period closes, so a practice head can see where capacity is sitting idle while there is still time to redeploy it. The value of utilization data is largely in its timeliness, and a governed, current view is what makes it a management tool rather than a scorecard.

## Everyone sees their own scope

A firm has partners, practice heads, and management, and they should not all see everything. **Row-level security** in the model handles this: a partner sees their matters and clients, a practice head sees their practice, management sees the firm, all from the same governed reports, with the rules defined once in the model and inherited everywhere. That is what lets one model serve the whole firm, from an individual timekeeper's utilization up to firm-wide realization, without building separate reports per role or worrying about who can see which client's economics.

## It scales down to smaller firms

There is an assumption that this kind of financial analytics is only for large firms with dedicated analysts. The opposite is often true: smaller and midsize firms feel the pain most, because the cost of manual reporting falls on people who should be practicing law, and there is no analyst to absorb it.

A focused Power BI model over the time, billing, and practice systems gives a small or midsize firm the same clear view of realization, utilization, and matter profitability that large firms build teams to produce, without the overhead. You start with the few views that run the firm and add depth as it grows. The underlying problem, scattered data and numbers that do not reconcile, is identical whether the firm has ten lawyers or five hundred, so the approach scales down well. The firm that sees its realization and matter profitability clearly, early, and consistently manages its economics far better than the one still assembling them by hand after the quarter has closed, and for a business that sells time, managing those economics is managing the firm.
