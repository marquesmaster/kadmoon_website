Every leadership meeting about trade has the same failure mode: finance brings one landed cost number, operations brings another, compliance has its own view, and the first twenty minutes are spent arguing about whose figure is right instead of deciding anything. A control tower exists to end that. It is not another dashboard; it is the one governed view where cost, service, and risk share a definition and reconcile, so the meeting is about the decision.

## Cost, service, and risk in one view

A trade operation is judged on three things at once: what it costs, how reliably goods move, and what risk it carries. A control tower brings them together, [landed cost](/blog/landed-cost-calculation) and [duty exposure](/blog/tariff-exposure-section-232-301) on the cost side, OTIF and dwell on the service side, screening and [classification](/blog/hts-classification-accuracy) on the risk side, in one place where they can be read against each other. Seeing them separately, in three tools owned by three teams, is how trade-offs get made blind.

## It is a governance problem, not a visual one

The reason control towers fail is that people build the visual before the model. A chart is easy; a chart everyone trusts is not. The control tower only works on a governed model where each KPI is defined once, so [landed cost](/docs/kpi-library) means the same thing in the executive view as in the operational report, and [effective duty rate](/blog/effective-duty-rate) is computed one way everywhere. Without that, you have prettier dashboards that still do not reconcile, and the meeting argument comes back.

## Every number drills to the shipment behind it

A summary number is only useful if you can get to what is under it. The test of a control tower is whether an executive can click an OTIF miss and see the lanes and shipments that caused it, or click a duty figure and see the entries and HTS codes behind it. That drill path is what turns a number from a talking point into something you can act on. It requires the summary and the detail to live on the same model, not in a slide copied from a report.

## Why spreadsheets cannot be a control tower

Spreadsheets and disconnected point tools can each show a number. What they cannot do is reconcile, because each is built on its own extract, at its own time, with its own definition. A control tower assembled from them is a snapshot that is wrong the moment the next invoice or milestone lands, and that no two teams compute the same way. The control tower is precisely the thing a pile of spreadsheets is not: one current, governed, drillable view.

## The executive layer of the analytics

Built correctly, a control tower is not a separate product; it is the top layer of the same [analytics](/platform) that already run landed cost, duty, visibility, and compliance, fed from the systems the operation already uses. The operational reports and the executive view share one model, so they never disagree. That is also why it is the last thing to build, not the first: it depends on the governed data underneath it being right.
