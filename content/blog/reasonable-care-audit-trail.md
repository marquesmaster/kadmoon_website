Customs law puts the responsibility for getting imports right on the importer, not the broker and not CBP. Under reasonable care, you must classify, value, and declare correctly, and you must keep the records to prove you did. That single principle changes what data quality means: a wrong number is not just an inefficiency, it is a potential failure of a legal duty. The defense is not perfection; it is being able to show the care you took, from every declared figure back to its source.

## Reasonable care is a legal duty

The reasonable-care standard makes the importer of record responsible for correct declarations. CBP informs and issues rulings, but the obligation to get [classification](/blog/hts-classification-accuracy), [valuation](/blog/customs-value-valuation), and declarations right sits with you. That matters most when something is wrong: CBP judges whether an error reflected reasonable care or a failure of it, and that judgment affects the consequences. An honest mistake inside a careful process is treated differently from a pattern of errors with no process behind it.

## The record is part of the requirement

Being right is not sufficient; you also have to be able to show how you got there. Importers must keep their import records, generally for five years, and produce them on request. That includes the documentation behind the decisions, the basis for a classification, the build-up of a customs value, not just the entry forms. A correct declaration with no supporting record is weaker than it looks, because the requirement is both the right answer and the ability to demonstrate it. Recordkeeping completeness is itself something an audit checks.

## Data quality becomes legal exposure

Once correctness and documentation are legal obligations, data quality stops being an operational nicety. A high [classification](/blog/hts-classification-accuracy) error rate, frequent post-summary corrections, inconsistent valuations, and decisions with no recorded rationale are not just messy, they are the signals that reasonable care may not be met, and they are precisely what an audit probes. The same errors that quietly cost duty also create compliance risk, which means cleaning them up pays twice: lower duty leakage and lower exposure.

## An audit trail is the defense

What makes a position defensible is lineage: the ability to trace every declared number back to the data and the decision behind it. When an auditor or a regulator asks why a good was classified or valued as it was, the answer should come from a record, not a memory. An [audit trail](/docs/security) that ties each entry to its source data and its documented rationale turns a question into a report. That traceability is the practical form reasonable care takes: not a claim that you were careful, but evidence of it.

## Measuring compliance health is analytics

The signals of reasonable care are measurable. Classification error and correction rates, valuation consistency, [screening coverage](/blog/restricted-party-screening-coverage), and documentation completeness are all views over the import data, the same data the [analytics](/platform) already use to model duty. Tracking them turns compliance health from an annual scramble before an audit into a number you watch, and keeping the lineage intact means the audit trail is a byproduct of the analytics, not a separate project. Reasonable care, measured, is just good data discipline with legal weight behind it.
