Sourcing decisions are usually made on the quoted unit price, because that is the number on the table. It is also the number most likely to mislead. Once freight, duty, lead time, and reliability are counted, the cheapest invoice often is not the cheapest supplier. Supplier analytics is the correction: scoring suppliers on what they actually cost and how dependable they are, so sourcing runs on evidence instead of on the price in the negotiation.

## Invoice price is not supplier cost

The quoted price is where comparison starts, not where it ends. A supplier's real cost includes the freight from their origin, the [duty](/blog/duty-optimization-four-levers) their country and product carry, and the operational cost of how they perform. Two suppliers quoting the same unit price can differ by double digits once those are added. Comparing on invoice price alone is comparing one component and calling it the total, which is exactly how a more expensive supplier wins on paper and loses in the accounts.

## Score on true landed cost

The first fix is to rank suppliers on [landed cost](/blog/landed-cost-calculation) per unit, with freight, duty, insurance, and fees allocated in, rather than on invoice price. That single change reorders most supplier lists, because freight and duty vary so much by origin. A supplier close to a cheap lane with a low duty profile can beat one with a lower quote but high freight and tariff exposure. Landed cost is the honest basis for the comparison, and it is already the number the rest of your [analytics](/platform) produce.

## Lead time and reliability are costs

Price is static; performance is not, and performance costs money. A supplier with a longer or more variable [lead time](/blog/forecast-accuracy-cross-border-inventory) ties up more inventory and forces more expedites, both real costs that never appear on the invoice. A supplier that short-ships or has quality rejections costs service and rework. Putting lead time, its variability, delivery reliability, and rejection rates into the scorecard turns "cheap but unreliable" from a vague worry into a measured cost you can weigh against the price.

## Duty profile is part of the choice

Because duty depends on country of origin and tariff exposure, it is a supplier attribute, not just a customs detail. The same good from two countries can carry very different base duty, [Section 301 or 232](/blog/tariff-exposure-section-232-301) tariffs, or AD/CVD. A supplier's duty profile can make them the cheapest or the most expensive option before freight is even counted, and when tariffs move, that ranking moves. Keeping duty exposure by supplier and origin in the sourcing view is what lets you see a tariff change as a sourcing decision, not just a cost increase.

## A scorecard turns sourcing into evidence

Put together, these make a supplier scorecard: landed cost per unit, lead time and variability, reliability, quality, and duty profile, tracked over time. That turns a sourcing conversation from a price negotiation into an evidence-based comparison, and it surfaces the suppliers quietly costing more than they quote. It is the same [trade analytics](/platform) that model cost and service, organized by supplier, which is where sourcing decisions are actually made.
