Most duty conversations are about the base rate and the tariff overlays. The duty that can actually break a product's economics is often neither: it is AD/CVD. Antidumping and countervailing duties apply to specific products from specific countries, sit on top of everything else, and can run from a few percent to several hundred. They are also the hardest duty to manage, because they are precise, producer-specific, and they change after you have already imported. Treating them as a footnote is how importers get a surprise bill.

## What they are, and why they are large

Antidumping duties offset goods sold below fair value; countervailing duties offset foreign subsidies. Commerce sets the margins, the USITC finds injury, and the result is an extra duty specific to a product, a country, and frequently an individual producer. Because they are meant to erase an unfair price advantage, the rates can be enormous, far beyond a normal tariff. They stack on the base duty and any [Section 301 or 232](/blog/tariff-exposure-section-232-301) tariffs, so a product caught by an order can carry a total duty that makes it uneconomic.

## They are precise, which makes them tricky

AD/CVD do not apply by broad category; they apply by scope. The order names a product, a country, and often specific producers with their own rates. Two goods that look the same can carry very different AD/CVD depending on origin and maker. That precision means exposure depends on exactly what you buy and from whom, down to the producer, and it means a sourcing change can move you into or out of an order. Managing it requires knowing your purchases at that level of detail, not at the HTS-chapter level.

## The retroactive bill

The feature that catches importers off guard is that the deposit is not the final duty. You pay a cash-deposit rate at entry, but AD/CVD entries stay open and are trued up at annual administrative reviews, which can revise the rate up or down and settle the difference, retroactively, on goods you imported months ago and may have already sold. A higher reviewed rate is a bill on the past. The [gap between deposited and final duty](/blog/effective-duty-rate), and the open liability while entries are unliquidated, is real financial exposure that a normal duty report does not show.

## Scope rulings change the map

Even the question of whether a product is subject to an order is not always settled. Scope rulings can determine that a good you believed was outside an order is actually within it, pulling it into AD/CVD overnight and sometimes retroactively. That means AD/CVD exposure is not just about the products you know are covered; it is also about the ones near the edge of an order's scope. Watching the orders that touch your product lines is part of managing the risk, not an occasional legal check.

## Measuring it is precise analytics

AD/CVD is where generic duty reporting fails and item-level analytics earns its place. The useful views are exposure by product, country, and producer; the deposit rates in effect; the open liability on unliquidated entries; and the proximity of your goods to the scope of active orders. That is a precise join across your purchases, your entries, and the order data, the same [customs analytics](/platform) that model duty, taken down to the producer level. Without it, AD/CVD is managed by hoping the next review does not move, which is not management.
