An HVAC or home services contractor makes its money one job and one technician at a time, and its profitability is shaped by the mix of work it does: service calls, installs and replacements, and the membership plans that tie customers in. Seeing all of that clearly is hard, because job, technician, and financial data live in field service software and financial systems that do not join, so the contractor tends to see the field software's operational view without the fuller performance picture. Power BI's role is to join those systems into one governed view, so revenue per job, technician productivity, and the service-versus-install mix are visible across the business. This is how home services contractors use it.

## The performance picture is buried in the field software

Ask a home services contractor a question that should drive the business, which of our technicians are really producing, which job types pay best, and how is the business actually doing, and the answer takes someone exporting from the field service software, pulling financials, and assembling it by hand. The field software holds jobs, technicians, and scheduling, finance holds revenue and cost, and the two do not join into a performance view. The contractor sees the operational picture, what is scheduled, what is done, but the deeper questions about productivity and mix are reconstructed manually and late, if at all.

The first and most valuable thing Power BI does is build a **governed model that joins these systems**, so revenue per job, technician productivity, and the service-versus-install mix can be read together by technician, job type, and over time. Power BI reads from the field service and dispatch software and the financial systems, and the model relates them into one coherent picture. Once that model exists, the performance questions that took manual assembly become reports the contractor opens. That join is the foundation, because the business's performance depends on connecting jobs to technicians to revenue, and no single system holds all of it.

## Technician productivity: the engine of the business

In a home services business, the **technicians are the engine**, and their productivity is where revenue is won and lost, so seeing it clearly is central. The spread between the most and least productive technicians is often large, and it is largely invisible when the data sits in the field software, where productivity is not something the operational screens are built to show. Yet that spread is one of the biggest factors in the contractor's results.

A governed model that shows **productivity by technician**, revenue produced, jobs completed, how each compares to the team, makes that spread visible and actionable. The contractor can see who is producing, who needs support or training, and what the top performers do differently, so it can lift the whole team toward the best rather than tolerating a wide gap it cannot see. For a business whose revenue is produced one technician at a time, seeing productivity clearly is directly seeing where the business is made, and managing it deliberately, coaching the middle, learning from the top, addressing the bottom, is one of the highest-leverage things a contractor can do. The field software runs the schedule; the governed model shows who is actually producing, which is a different and more valuable view.

## The mix: service, install, and memberships

A home services contractor's profitability is shaped heavily by its **mix of work**, and the mix is hidden until the data is joined. **Service and maintenance calls**, **install and replacement jobs**, and **membership-plan work** each carry different economics and play different roles. Service often feeds installs, a maintenance visit surfaces the aging system that becomes a replacement; memberships smooth revenue and build loyalty; installs are large but lumpy. A contractor looking only at total revenue cannot see how this mix is driving its results, or whether the mix is healthy.

A governed model that shows **revenue and margin by job type** lets a contractor understand its mix and manage it deliberately. It can see how much comes from high-margin work, how well service is feeding the install pipeline, and what memberships are contributing in recurring value and retention, and balance the mix toward what makes the business strong rather than taking whatever comes through the door. This matters because the same total revenue can be made of very different mixes with very different profitability and durability, and only seeing the mix lets the contractor steer toward the better one. Memberships in particular, the recurring, loyalty-building core many strong contractors build around, are easy to undervalue when their contribution is not visible; the governed view makes their role clear, so the contractor can grow the part of the business that smooths and sustains it.

## It fits contractors of any size

A smaller contractor benefits as clearly as a large one, because even a few trucks generate job, technician, and financial data that lives in the field software and gets reconciled by hand, usually by an owner also running the business. Bringing it into one governed view gives a smaller contractor a clear picture of revenue per job, technician productivity, and job mix without a data team, through a partner who builds it and a managed arrangement that keeps it running. Larger contractors run more trucks and technicians, but the core value, seeing productivity and mix clearly, applies at any size, and the underlying problem, performance buried in the field software, is the same whether you run two trucks or twenty.

## What home services contractors end up with

An HVAC or home services contractor running on Power BI ends up with:

- Field service and financial data joined so performance can be read, not just operations.
- Technician productivity visible, so the spread that drives results becomes clear and manageable.
- The service, install, and membership mix seen by margin, so the business can be steered toward the right work.
- A clear current view without the manual assembly from the field software or a data team.

The throughline is that a home services contractor stops reading only the operational view in the field software and piecing performance together by hand, and starts seeing revenue per job, technician productivity, and job mix on one governed, current view. The productivity spread that makes or loses the business becomes visible and manageable, the work mix becomes something steered rather than accepted, and the contractor runs on evidence. For a business built one job and one technician at a time, seeing who produces and what pays is what turns the scattered field and financial data into the decisions that grow revenue per truck and build a more profitable, durable business.
