Trade policy has stopped being a slow-moving backdrop. Section 232 scope expanded repeatedly through 2025 and 2026, Section 301 tariffs stayed in force, and other overlays came and went. For an importer, the practical problem is not reading the news; it is knowing, the day a rate changes, what it costs you. That is tariff exposure, and most operations cannot answer it quickly because their duty lives in a spreadsheet that is always a step behind.

## Tariffs stack, and the total is what you pay

The base duty in the [tariff schedule](/docs/glossary) is only the start. On top of it can sit Section 301 duties, Section 232 duties, antidumping and countervailing duties, and any current overlay, and they stack. The number that actually matters is the effective duty rate: total duties and fees over total entered value. Two products with the same base rate can carry very different effective rates once the special tariffs are applied, and that difference is invisible if you only look at the schedule.

## Section 232 moves by product and origin

Section 232 is the clearest example of why this has to be tracked, not memorized. Its covered list expanded well beyond the original steel and aluminum through 2025 and 2026, the rates differ by article type, and it is assessed by product and origin. Every time a proclamation changes the scope, some of your lines move and others do not. A static assumption about what is covered goes stale quickly, so the covered mapping has to be maintained and applied to your actual catalog. (Always confirm the current scope and rates against the proclamations before relying on them in a filing.)

## Exposure, not the headline rate, is the decision input

A headline rate is a policy fact. Exposure is a business number: the dollars of duty at risk by product line, HTS, and origin. It tells you where the impact concentrates and what a change is worth. Most of the exposure in a catalog usually sits in a handful of lines, and those are the ones worth modeling, re-sourcing, or [deferring duty](/docs/glossary) on. Without the breakdown, every rate change looks equally urgent, which means none of them get the right response.

## Modeling a change before it lands

The point of measuring exposure is to act before the duty is paid. With duty allocated to the unit and each tariff identified separately, a proposed rate can be applied to the affected lines to read the change in total duty and in [landed cost](/blog/landed-cost-calculation). That turns a policy announcement into a concrete choice: reprice the product, shift sourcing or origin, change mode, or move volume into a structure that defers or reduces duty. The operations that do this are deciding; the ones that wait are absorbing.

## Why this is an analytics problem

Doing it once in a spreadsheet is feasible. Keeping it current is not, because the inputs change constantly: entries arrive, classifications get corrected, and rates move by proclamation. A governed model that holds duty by HTS and origin, separates the tariff stack, and refreshes as entries and rates change is what makes "what is our exposure if this rate goes up" a same-day answer.
