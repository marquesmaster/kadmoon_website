For most of its life, USMCA was a stable assumption: qualifying goods from Mexico and Canada moved duty-free, and the rules were known. The July 2026 joint review changed the posture. The United States declined to renew the agreement in its current form, which under its own mechanics triggers annual reviews rather than ending it. The agreement remains in force, but specific terms, including some of the most valuable rules of origin, are now subjects of negotiation. For importers who rely on preference, that turns a settled fact into something to monitor.

## What the review actually did

The review did not terminate USMCA. It moved the three countries into a process of annual reviews, with the agreement still in force and a scheduled expiry in 2036 if nothing ends it sooner, while bilateral talks continue. The uncertainty is about terms, not existence. The items reported as under negotiation, automotive [rules of origin](/docs/glossary) and steel and aluminum content, matter precisely because they decide which goods qualify and how much duty preference saves. (As with all fast-moving trade policy, verify the current state before relying on it in a filing.)

## Preference is only worth what you claim

An FTA does not save money on its own. It saves money on the qualifying goods you actually certify and claim. The real metric is the FTA qualification rate: the share of eligible value you claim preference on, against the duty you leave on the table by not qualifying goods you could have. Many operations claim less than they could because qualification is work, and some claim on goods that would not survive scrutiny, which is its own risk. Both gaps are measurable, and both cost money.

## Qualification is a data calculation

Deciding whether a good qualifies is not a judgment call; it is a calculation against the specific rule for its classification. That means a tariff-shift test, a [regional value content](/docs/glossary) calculation, or product-specific rules, applied to the bill of materials and the origin of every component. It has to be redone whenever the bill of materials changes, sourcing moves, or the rule itself is revised. Done by hand across a real product range, it falls behind; done as a data calculation, it stays current and produces the documentation the claim needs.

## Why the annual-review posture raises the stakes

When the rules were stable, a qualification done once held. In an annual-review posture, the rules themselves can move, so qualification has to be something you can re-run against a changed rule and read the impact. The question "if the automotive rule tightens, which of our goods stop qualifying, and what does that cost in duty" is a modeling question, and answering it is how an importer prepares instead of reacts.

## What to track now

Three views carry most of the value: the qualification rate and the duty saved versus available; the goods whose qualification is marginal and therefore most at risk from a rule change; and the duty exposure that appears if preference is lost on a given flow.
