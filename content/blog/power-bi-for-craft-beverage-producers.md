A craft beverage producer, a brewery, winery, distillery, or cidery, makes a physical product on a schedule set well ahead of demand, and sells it through channels that pay very differently. Running it well means seeing what it makes, where that product actually sells, and what each channel is worth, together, and that is hard, because production, distribution, and direct sales live in separate systems that do not connect. Power BI's role for a producer is to join those systems into one governed view, so production, sales, and channel margin are visible together, and the producer can decide what to make and where to sell it on evidence. This is how craft beverage producers use it.

## Production and sales live apart

Ask a craft producer a question that should tie the business together, what are we making, where is it actually selling, and which channel is earning us the most, and the answer takes someone pulling production records, exporting distributor and wholesale sales, checking the tasting room or online store, and assembling it by hand. Production systems hold what was made, distribution holds the wholesale movement, point-of-sale holds the direct sales, and none of it connects. The producer sees production in one place and sales in another, and the link between them, which product sells where and at what return, is reconstructed manually if at all.

The first and most valuable thing Power BI does is build a **governed model that joins these systems**, so production, sales, and channel performance can be read together by product, channel, and over time. Power BI reads from production, distribution, and direct-sales sources, and the model relates them into one coherent picture. Once that model exists, the questions that tie making to selling become reports the producer opens. That join is the foundation, because a producer's real decisions depend on connecting what it makes to where that product sells and what it returns, and no single system holds both halves.

## The channels pay very differently

A craft producer sells through channels with very different economics, and **channel mix** is where a lot of the real profitability hides, because the difference stays invisible until the channels are joined. Selling through a **distributor**, selling to **retail**, and selling **direct** through a tasting room, cellar door, or online store carry very different margins. The same case or bottle is worth markedly different amounts depending on where it goes, so a producer's profit depends not just on how much it sells but on **where** it sells.

A producer looking at total sales sees the volume but not the margin behind it. A governed model that joins the channels lets the producer see what each is actually worth, so it understands the true profitability of its sales and can favor the channels that return most. Most producers find that direct sales carry the best margin, which makes the tasting room or online store not just a brand experience but the most profitable channel, worth growing deliberately. Seeing the channel economics is what turns sales **volume** into an understanding of where the **money** actually is, which is a different and more useful thing, and it changes how a producer thinks about where to push its product.

## Making what the business needs

A craft producer commits to **production ahead of demand**, and getting the mix wrong is expensive: cash ties up in product that moves slowly, while the best sellers run short and leave margin on the table. The hard part is that production planning needs to be informed by where products actually sell and what they return, which is exactly the connection the scattered systems hide.

A governed model that shows **sales by product and channel against production and inventory** lets the producer plan what to make on evidence. It can see what is selling, through which channels, at what margin, and which products linger, and shape production toward the real picture: more of what sells well in the channels that pay best, less of what sits. This matching of production to actual demand and margin is where a producer protects both its **cash** and its **profitability**, because cash tied up in slow product is cash not working, and a shortfall of the best sellers is margin missed. For a business that must decide what to make before it knows how sales will land, connecting production to real demand and channel returns is the core of what the analytics provides, turning a committed guess into an informed plan.

## It fits producers of any size

A small producer benefits as clearly as a large one, often most, because it runs the same spread of production, distribution, and direct sales with far less time to make sense of it, usually falling on the owner who is also making the product. A focused Power BI model over the producer's data gives a small brewery or winery a clear view of what it makes, where it sells, and what each channel returns, without a data team. Larger producers run more products and channels, but the core value, connecting production to sales and seeing channel margin, applies at any size, and for a small producer deciding what to make and where to sell it with real money committed, that clarity is exactly what it needs.

## What craft beverage producers end up with

A craft beverage producer running on Power BI ends up with:

- Production, distribution, and direct-sales data joined so making and selling connect.
- Channel margin visible, so the producer sees what each channel is really worth.
- Production planned against real demand and margin, protecting cash and profitability.
- A clear view of what to make and where to sell it, without a data team.

The throughline is that a craft beverage producer stops reading production and sales in separate reports and starts seeing how what it makes connects to where it sells and what each channel returns. The channel economics become clear, so the producer favors the sales that pay best, and production gets planned against real demand rather than guessed. For a business that commits to making a physical product ahead of demand and sells it through channels that pay very differently, connecting production to sales and seeing where the margin actually is turns the scattered data into the two decisions a producer lives by: what to make, and where to sell it.
