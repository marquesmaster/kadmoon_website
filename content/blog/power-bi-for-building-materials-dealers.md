A building materials or lumber dealer runs a trade where costs move fast, margins are thin, and much of the business flows through contractor accounts on negotiated pricing and terms. Its results depend on seeing real margin as costs swing, keeping its inventory position clear, and knowing which accounts actually make money, and all of that is hard, because sales, inventory, and account data live in separate systems and the numbers move quickly. Power BI's role is to join those systems into one governed view, so real margin, inventory, and account profitability are visible on current data. This is how building materials dealers use it.

## Fast-moving numbers, scattered systems

Ask a dealer the questions that decide profitability, what is our real margin right now, what is our inventory position, and which accounts actually make money, and the answer takes someone pulling from the ERP, inventory, and account systems and assembling it by hand, by which point the fast-moving numbers have moved. The ERP holds sales, inventory holds stock, account systems hold the contractor pricing and terms, and none of it joins into a current view of margin and profitability. The dealer sees pieces, and the picture that matters, real margin against today's cost, inventory position, account profitability, is reconstructed manually and already stale.

The first and most valuable thing Power BI does is build a **governed model that joins these systems**, so real margin, inventory position, and account profitability can be read together by product, account, and over time. Power BI reads from the ERP, inventory, and account sources, and the model relates them into one coherent, current picture. Once that model exists, the fast-moving questions become reports the dealer opens. That join is the foundation, because dealer profitability depends on connecting sales, at margin against volatile cost, to inventory and accounts, and no single system shows that, in a trade where speed matters because the numbers do not sit still.

## Real margin in a volatile trade

Building materials costs, especially **commodities like lumber**, **swing sharply**, which makes margins **thin and fast-moving**, so **real-time margin visibility** is essential because **yesterday's cost is not today's.** A dealer that cannot see real current margin, selling price against **actual current cost**, can be selling at prices that no longer cover cost after a swing, losing margin without realizing it until the period closes and the numbers disappoint. In a volatile trade, pricing on stale cost assumptions is a direct path to margin erosion.

A governed model that shows **real margin against current cost** lets a dealer see where it is actually making money **as costs move.** With that visible, the dealer can price and manage **in time**, adjusting as commodity costs swing, spotting products whose margin has eroded, and protecting profitability against the volatility rather than discovering the damage after the fact. For a thin-margin trade on volatile-cost inventory, this current margin visibility is central, because the margin is both slim and constantly shifting under the dealer's feet, and only seeing real margin against today's cost reveals whether the business is actually profitable right now. A dealer that can see current margin manages a volatile trade on evidence; one that cannot is pricing on costs that may already be obsolete, which in this trade is dangerous.

## Contractor accounts: profitability, not just revenue

Much of a dealer's business flows through **contractor accounts** on negotiated **pricing and terms**, and those accounts vary widely in **profitability** once pricing, terms, and volume are counted, which account revenue alone hides. A **large** account on **tight pricing** with **extended terms** can be **less profitable** than a **smaller** one at better margins, but looking at account **revenue**, the large account looks far more important. Revenue ranks accounts by size; profitability ranks them by what they are actually worth, and the two can diverge sharply.

A governed model that shows **profitability by account**, real margin against the pricing and terms each account gets, lets a dealer see which accounts actually make money and which do not. With that visible, it can manage **pricing, terms, and focus** by what each account is really worth: renegotiating unprofitable large accounts, protecting quietly profitable ones, and directing attention by profitability rather than revenue. Seeing account profitability is how a dealer manages its **book** of contractor relationships deliberately, because in a thin-margin trade, a few unprofitable large accounts can **absorb the margin** of many good ones, and only seeing profitability, not revenue, reveals that. The contractor book is central to the dealer's business, and managing it by true profitability rather than size is key to making the whole operation pay.

## It fits dealers of any size

A smaller dealer benefits as clearly as a large chain, because even a single yard runs sales, inventory, and account data in separate systems reconciled by hand, and volatile margins and account profitability matter just as much. Bringing it into one governed view gives a smaller dealer a clear, current picture without a data team, through a partner who builds it and a managed arrangement that keeps it running. Larger chains run more locations and SKUs, but the core value, seeing real margin, inventory, and account profitability on current data, applies at any size, and the underlying challenge is the same whether you run one yard or many.

## What building materials dealers end up with

A building materials or lumber dealer running on Power BI ends up with:

- ERP, inventory, and account data joined so real margin and profitability can be read together, currently.
- Real margin against current cost visible, so volatile-trade pricing is managed in time.
- Inventory position clear, so stock is managed against fast-moving demand and cost.
- Contractor account profitability seen, so the book is managed by what accounts are really worth.

The throughline is that a building materials dealer stops pricing and managing on stale, scattered numbers and starts seeing real margin, inventory, and account profitability on one governed, current view, in a trade where costs and stock move fast. The margin against today's cost becomes visible so pricing keeps up with the volatility, the inventory position stays clear, and the contractor book gets managed by profitability rather than size. For a business with thin margins on volatile-cost inventory sold through accounts of varying profitability, seeing all of that on current data is what turns the scattered sales and account data into the decisions that protect margin in a trade that does not sit still.
