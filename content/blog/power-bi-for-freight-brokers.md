A freight broker makes its money in the spread: the difference between what a customer pays to move a load and what it costs to pay a carrier to haul it. That spread is thin, it varies load to load, and the business moves fast, so a broker's results depend on seeing real margin per load, how its carriers perform, and which customers actually make money. Seeing all of that clearly is hard, because load, carrier, and financial data live in the TMS and financial systems that do not give a clear margin and profitability view. Power BI's role is to join those systems into one governed view, so load margin, carrier performance, and customer profitability are visible in a fast, thin-margin business. This is how freight brokers use it.

## Thin, fast margins across systems

Ask a broker the questions that decide profitability, what is our real margin by load and lane, which carriers perform, and which customers actually make money, and the answer takes someone pulling from the TMS and financials and assembling it by hand, by which point the fast-moving business has moved on. The TMS holds loads, carriers, and lanes, finance holds revenue and cost, and none of it joins into a view of real margin and profitability. The broker sees pieces and knows the spread is thin, but **where** the margin is made and lost is reconstructed manually and late.

The first and most valuable thing Power BI does is build a **governed model that joins these systems**, so margin per load, carrier performance, and customer profitability can be read together by load, lane, carrier, and customer. Power BI reads from the TMS and financial sources, and the model relates them into one coherent picture. Once that model exists, the questions about real margin and profitability become reports the broker opens. That join is the foundation, because brokerage profitability depends on connecting what a load earned to what it cost to cover and who it was for, and no single system shows that, in a business where margins are thin enough that small differences decide whether it adds up.

## Margin per load: where profitability is managed

Because brokerage margin is **thin and varies widely** load to load, seeing **real margin by load, lane, and customer** is where profitability is managed. A broker's margin is the **spread** between what the customer pays and what the carrier costs, which is **slim** and moves with the market, so a broker that cannot see real margin can be moving **many loads** at margins that do not add up to a healthy business, busy but not profitable, without knowing which lanes and customers carry it and which drag it down.

A governed model that shows **margin by load, lane, and customer** lets a broker see which **lanes and customers** actually pay and which do not. With that visible, the broker can price and manage deliberately, focusing on the lanes and customers that carry good margin, addressing the ones that do not, and understanding where its profit genuinely comes from. In a thin-margin business, this is the core of **staying profitable**, because the margin is slim and variable, so a broker that cannot see it is flying blind on the exact number that determines whether the business works. Seeing real margin by load, lane, and customer turns a high-volume, thin-margin operation from a hope that the spread adds up into a managed business where the broker knows which of its activity actually pays.

## Carrier performance: margin and service

A broker's **carriers** drive both its **margin** and the **service** it delivers, so carrier performance, in **cost** and **reliability**, is central, and the governed view makes it visible. Carriers vary in what they **cost**, which drives margin, and in how **reliably** they deliver on time and without problems, which drives whether the broker's customer gets the service promised. Both matter, and a broker effectively delivers through its carriers, so a carrier that is expensive or unreliable hurts the broker directly, in margin and in the customer relationship.

A governed model that shows **carrier performance** lets a broker see which carriers are **cost-effective and reliable**, so it can direct loads to the best ones and manage the rest. With that visible, it can protect **margin** by covering loads with cost-effective carriers and protect **service** by relying on carriers that deliver, while managing or avoiding those that do not. Seeing carrier performance clearly is how a broker manages the partners its own results depend on, which is central to brokerage, because the broker's product is effectively the carrier's performance, and a broker that cannot see which carriers perform cannot reliably protect either its margin or the service it sells. Managing carriers on evidence of cost and reliability is a large part of running a brokerage well.

## Customer profitability: managing the book

A broker's **customers** vary widely in **profitability** once **real margin and the effort to serve** them are counted, and that is hidden behind revenue. A **large** customer on **thin margins** with **heavy service demands** can be **less profitable** than a **smaller** one at better margins with little fuss, but looking at **revenue**, the large customer looks far more important. Revenue ranks customers by size; profitability ranks them by what they are actually worth.

A governed model that shows **profitability by customer**, real margin against the effort to serve, lets a broker see which customers actually make money and which do not. With that visible, it can manage **pricing, focus, and terms** by what each customer is really worth, repricing or reconsidering unprofitable large customers, protecting quietly profitable ones, and directing effort by profitability rather than revenue. Seeing customer profitability is how a broker manages its **book** deliberately, because in a thin-margin business, a few **unprofitable large customers** can **absorb the margin** of many good ones, and only seeing profitability, not revenue, reveals that. The customer book is the broker's business, and managing it by true profitability rather than size is central to making the whole thin-margin operation pay.

## It fits brokers of any size

A smaller broker benefits as clearly as a large one, because even a modest brokerage runs load, carrier, and financial data in separate systems reconciled by hand, and thin margins, carrier performance, and customer profitability matter just as much. Bringing it into one governed view gives a smaller broker a clear picture without a data team, through a partner who builds it and a managed arrangement that keeps it running. Larger brokers run more loads and carriers, but the core value, seeing real margin, carrier performance, and customer profitability, applies at any size, and the underlying economics, a thin, variable spread on high volume, are the same whatever the scale.

## What freight brokers end up with

A freight broker running on Power BI ends up with:

- TMS and financial data joined so real margin and profitability can be read together.
- Margin per load, lane, and customer visible, so the thin spread is managed on evidence.
- Carrier performance in cost and reliability visible, so margin and service are protected.
- Customer profitability seen, so the book is managed by what customers are really worth.

The throughline is that a freight broker stops moving loads on a thin, invisible spread and starts seeing real margin, carrier performance, and customer profitability on one governed view, where the thin line between profit and loss is actually managed. The margin per load becomes visible so the broker knows which activity pays, the carriers that drive both margin and service get managed on evidence, and the customer book gets managed by profitability rather than size. For a business that lives on a slim, variable spread at high volume, seeing all of that clearly is what turns the scattered load and carrier data into the decisions that keep a fast, thin-margin brokerage genuinely profitable.
