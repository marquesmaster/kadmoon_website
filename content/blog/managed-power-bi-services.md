Most Power BI problems do not show up on launch day. The dashboards go live, the executives are happy, and then the slow decay begins: a refresh fails silently on a Monday, a gateway credential expires, a new business question needs a measure nobody has time to write, and six months later half the reports are quietly distrusted again. Managed Power BI services exist to stop that decay, keeping your reporting healthy and evolving after the project ends. This guide covers what a good managed service actually includes, how a modern BI support retainer is priced, and when it beats the alternatives.

## What managed Power BI services include

A managed Power BI service is ongoing ownership of your reporting estate, not a break-fix hotline. The core of it is operational health: monitoring [dataset refreshes](https://learn.microsoft.com/power-bi/connect-data/refresh-data) and on-premises gateways, catching failures before users do, and keeping capacity and performance in check. On top of that sits governance that has to be maintained, not just installed once: [row-level security](https://learn.microsoft.com/power-bi/enterprise/service-admin-rls) roles, [Microsoft Purview](https://learn.microsoft.com/purview/) lineage and cataloging, and data loss prevention as your data and audience change.

The part that separates a real service from a support desk is forward motion. A good managed service ships new reports, new measures, and model improvements every month, so the platform keeps pace with the business instead of freezing at go-live. The best ones add a monthly review where you look at what shipped, what is slow or fragile, and what to build next, which is how a data platform stays trusted over years rather than months.

## Priced by capacity, not by the hour

The way a managed service is priced tells you whether it will last. Hourly billing punishes you for asking questions and turns every request into a negotiation. Truly "unlimited" retainers have the opposite failure: once a client leans on them, the provider's margin evaporates and delivery quietly slows to protect it.

The sustainable model in between is capacity-based. You pay a fixed monthly fee that buys a defined pool of hours and a response SLA, and you request work freely within those hours. It behaves like a subscription to a data team rather than a metered utility: predictable for you, sustainable for the provider, and honest about the one thing that actually constrains delivery, which is people's time. Tiers then differ by how much capacity and how fast an SLA you need, from keeping an existing estate healthy to running an active roadmap of new development. ## Managed service vs. hiring vs. projects

Three ways to keep Power BI alive, and they suit different situations.

- **A managed service** gives you a multidisciplinary team on a monthly subscription: modeling, data engineering, and governance under one SLA, without recruiting or managing anyone. It scales up when you need more and down when you do not.
- **Hiring in-house** makes sense when you need a full-time, deeply embedded owner and have enough steady work to justify it. The trade-off is that one hire is one skill set and a single point of failure, at well over one hundred thousand dollars a year fully loaded in the US.
- **One-off projects** are right for a bounded build or a [migration](/tableau-to-power-bi-migration), but they end, and the estate still needs someone to keep it healthy afterward.

Most teams that already have Power BI in production do not need another project; they need [sustained ownership](/services/bi-sustainment) with room to keep building.

## What good looks like

Not every "managed service" is worth buying. Four things separate the ones that keep earning their fee:

1. **A real SLA.** A named response time for issues and a clear escalation path, not a best-effort inbox.
2. **Governance that ships with the work.** Security, lineage, and DLP maintained as part of the service, so an audit is a non-event rather than a fire drill.
3. **Your tenant, your ownership.** Everything built and kept in your own Microsoft environment, with documentation, so there is no lock-in.
4. **Senior people doing the work.** The person who scopes your reports should be the one who builds them. Juniors on your budget is where quality and trust leak away.

## How to choose a provider

Ask three questions before you sign. First, how is the work priced, and what happens when you exceed the plan: the honest answer is a defined pool of hours with a pre-agreed way to add more, not vague "unlimited." Second, where does the work live and who owns it: it should be your tenant, with documentation handed over. Third, who actually does the delivery: the senior, [Microsoft-certified people](/about) who scoped it, or a rotating cast of juniors.
