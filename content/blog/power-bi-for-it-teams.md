An IT function runs on three things: how well it delivers service, what it manages across the estate, and what it all costs. Seeing these clearly is essential to running IT well, and it is harder than it should be, because the data lives in service management tools, asset systems, cloud billing, and monitoring that do not reconcile, and because cloud cost in particular hides in a level of detail that no single bill makes sense of. Power BI's role for an IT team is to join those systems into one governed view, so service performance, the estate, and spending are visible and managed as a whole. This is how IT teams use it.

## IT is split across its own tools

Ask an IT leader a question that spans the function, how is service performing, what does our estate look like, and where is our cloud spend going, and watch how many tools it takes to answer. Service management knows the tickets and SLAs. Asset and configuration tools know the estate. Cloud billing knows the spend, in overwhelming detail. Monitoring knows performance. Each holds a piece, none reconciles with the others, and the whole picture of the function has to be reconstructed by hand each time.

The first and most valuable thing Power BI does is build a **governed model that joins these tools**, so service performance, the asset estate, and cost can be read together. Power BI reads from the service management, asset, cloud cost, and monitoring systems, and the model relates them into one coherent picture. Once that model exists, the questions that used to take reconstructing across tools become reports you open. That join is the foundation, because the questions that matter to an IT leader span the tools, and no single one holds the whole picture of the function.

## Cloud spend, finally visible

The area where this most obviously pays off is **cloud cost**, because cloud spend is notoriously hard to see clearly. Cloud bills are spread across services, accounts, and resources in a level of detail that obscures rather than reveals, so organizations often genuinely cannot see where their cloud money actually goes, despite spending heavily on it.

A governed model that brings the cloud cost data together, and joins it to the **context of what the spend supports**, surfaces the real picture: which services, teams, or workloads drive the cost, where there is waste, and how spend is trending. This is the same visibility problem that afflicts procurement spend, in an even more detailed and fast-moving form, and the same solution applies: bring the scattered, overly-detailed data together and classify it consistently, so the real picture emerges from the noise.

That visibility is the foundation of managing cloud cost, because you cannot control spend you cannot see, and a raw cloud bill hides far more than it reveals. Once cloud spend is visible by service, team, and workload, IT can find the waste, attribute the cost, and manage it down, none of which is possible when the spend is buried in billing detail nobody can parse. For many IT functions, finally seeing cloud spend clearly is where the analytics most directly pays for itself, because cloud cost is large and, unmanaged, grows.

## Service tied to cost and impact

Service performance metrics, incident volumes, resolution times, SLA attainment, are standard IT fare, but they are far more useful **tied to cost and business impact** than measured in isolation. A metric on its own tells you how IT is performing in its own terms; connected to what it costs and what it affects, it tells you what the performance is worth to the business.

A governed model that joins service performance to cost and to the business context lets an IT leader read service not just as a set of operational metrics but in terms that matter to the organization: what a service issue costs, how the estate relates to incidents, where service problems affect the business. This connects IT's performance to the business it serves, which is what lets IT be managed as a value-delivering function rather than a cost center measured on internal metrics. Tying service to cost and impact, rather than reporting it in isolation, is what makes the service metrics genuinely useful to decisions about where to invest and what to improve.

## Complementing, not replacing, the tools

It is worth being clear that this complements IT's existing tools rather than replacing them. The service management tool, the asset system, the cloud console, each reports its own slice, and those slices are useful. What they do not provide is the **connected, cross-tool view**, because each sees only its own domain.

A governed Power BI model joins the slices so IT sees the whole picture, service, estate, and cost together, and can answer the questions that cross tools, which are exactly the questions that matter most for managing the function. It does not replace the service management tool or the cloud console; it sits above them, reading from each, to give the connected view none of them can provide alone. For an IT leader, that connected view is what turns a collection of tool-specific reports into a coherent picture of the function.

## What IT teams end up with

An IT team running on Power BI ends up with:

- Service management, asset, and cloud cost data joined so the function can be read as a whole.
- Cloud spend made visible by service, team, and workload, so waste can be found and cost managed.
- Service performance tied to its cost and business impact, rather than measured in isolation.
- A connected view across tools that complements the service management and cloud tools rather than replacing them.

The throughline is that an IT function stops reconstructing its picture by hand across separate tools and starts managing on one governed, connected view of service, estate, and spend. Cloud cost becomes visible and manageable, service performance connects to what it costs and affects, and IT is run as a coherent function rather than a set of tool-specific metrics. For a function that manages complex systems and significant, fast-growing cost, seeing service, estate, and spend together and clearly is what turns the data IT's own tools already generate into decisions that improve service and control cost.
