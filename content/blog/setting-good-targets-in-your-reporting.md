Open a report and you see numbers: revenue, conversion, cost, volume. Each tells you **where you are.** But none of them, on its own, tells you the thing you actually want to know: is that **good?** Should you be pleased or concerned? A number answers "what is it?" but not "is it where it should be?", and that second question is the one that drives action. **Targets** answer it. A target gives a number meaning, turning a bare value into a signal you can respond to. Setting targets well is a large part of what turns a report from a readout into a tool for managing. Here is how to think about it.

## A number alone does not tell you enough

Start with the limitation of a bare number. Revenue of a certain amount is just a value. Is it good? You cannot tell, not without knowing what it **should** be. The same is true of a conversion rate, a cost figure, a volume: each tells you **where you are**, and leaves open the question of whether where you are is where you want to be. Without that, the number is informative but not **actionable**, because action comes from the gap between where you are and where you should be, and a bare number shows only the first half.

A **target** supplies the other half. It says what the number **should** be, so the comparison between actual and target turns the value into a **signal**: on track or off, ahead or behind. That signal is what people can **respond to.** Nobody can act on "revenue is X"; people can act on "revenue is X, which is below where it should be." The target is what converts a number from something you **read** into something you can **manage by**, which is the difference between a report that describes and a report that drives. A metric without a target is a readout; a metric with a good target is a tool.

## What makes a target good

Not every target does this job, though. A target only gives a number useful meaning if it is set well, and good targets share three properties.

**Realistic.** A good target is attainable enough that people take it **seriously.** A target that is obviously unreachable, or trivially easy, gets dismissed, and a dismissed target provides no useful signal, because nobody treats being off it as meaningful. Realistic targets are believed, and believed targets drive behavior.

**Owned.** A good target is **owned by someone**, a person or team responsible for the number and its target. Ownership is what makes being off target prompt **action** rather than a collective shrug. A number nobody owns is a number nobody responds to when it slips; an owned number has someone who notices and acts. Ownership turns the signal into a response.

**Tied to a decision or action.** A good target is connected to something that will be **done differently** depending on whether it is hit or missed. If missing a target changes nothing, the target is decorative; if missing it triggers a response, scrutiny, a change of approach, resources, the target is doing its job. The point of a target is to drive action, so a target disconnected from any action is just a number pretending to matter.

Targets that are **arbitrary** (round numbers nobody believes), **unowned** (nobody responsible), or **disconnected** (nothing happens either way) fail these tests and get ignored. This is why **how** a target is set matters as much as **that** one exists: a target that is realistic, owned, and tied to action gives a number real meaning, while one that is not gives only the appearance of it.

## A bad target is worse than none

It is worth stating plainly: a **bad target can be worse than no target**, because of what it teaches. An **arbitrary** target that is obviously a round number nobody believes, or a target nobody **owns** or **acts on**, trains people that the target is **meaningless.** And once people learn to ignore **one** target, they tend to ignore **others**, including the good ones. The habit of disregarding targets, once learned, spreads.

So a report full of **arbitrary** targets can be **worse** than a report with none, because it looks like management-by-target while actually **conditioning everyone to disregard targets.** The targets are there, visibly, but they have taught the organization to tune them out, which is harder to undo than simply not having them. This is why setting targets carelessly does **real harm**, not just no good: it burns the credibility that makes any target useful. The lesson is to set **fewer, meaningful** targets rather than **many hollow** ones. A handful of targets that are realistic, owned, and acted on will drive the business; a wall of arbitrary ones will train everyone to ignore the wall.

## Targets make reporting a management tool

Pulling it together: good targets are what turn reporting from a **readout** into a **management tool.** A report that just shows numbers **describes** the business, telling you what is. A report that shows numbers against **meaningful targets** gives you a basis to **manage** the business, telling you what is **against what should be**, and directing attention to the gaps. That comparison is what people manage by, because it points to where performance is off and prompts action there, which a bare description cannot do.

This is why target-setting is not a trivial add-on but a real part of making reporting useful. Numbers against good targets focus attention and drive response; numbers alone just sit there, and numbers against bad targets actively erode the practice. Setting targets well, **realistic, owned, tied to action**, so that being off target reliably leads to someone doing something, is a meaningful part of what makes reporting something that **drives how the business is run** rather than something people glance at and move on from. A good partner helps set targets that work this way, because the best model and cleanest reports still only describe the business until the numbers are given the meaning that targets provide. The report shows where you are; the targets are what make it matter.

## The point in short

Targets are what give numbers meaning and make reports actionable:

- A number alone shows **where you are**, not whether that is **good**; a target supplies the comparison.
- Good targets are **realistic, owned, and tied to a decision or action**.
- **Bad targets** (arbitrary, unowned, disconnected) are **worse than none**, because they train people to ignore targets.
- Good targets turn a report from a **readout** into a **management tool** by comparing what is to what should be.

The throughline is that a metric without a target answers "what is it?" but leaves unanswered "is that good?", which is the question that drives action, and good targets answer it. Setting them well, realistic, owned, and connected to what people will actually do, is what turns reporting from a description of the business into a tool for managing it. It is a simple idea with real consequences: the same numbers, given meaningful targets, become something an organization acts on, and given hollow ones, become noise everyone learns to ignore.
