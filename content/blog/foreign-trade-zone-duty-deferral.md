When duty was a few points, the structures that defer or reduce it were a nice-to-have for specialized flows. As tariffs rose through 2025 and 2026, the math changed. Deferring duty on inventory, eliminating it on re-exports, and capping fees are worth far more when the duty being deferred is larger. [Foreign-Trade Zones](/docs/glossary) and bonded warehouses are the main tools, and the question for most importers is no longer whether they exist but whether one pays off for their specific flow.

## What an FTZ does

A Foreign-Trade Zone sits inside the United States but is treated as outside customs territory for duty purposes. Goods can be admitted, stored, handled, or manufactured in the zone, and duty is assessed only when they are withdrawn into U.S. commerce. That single fact drives the benefits: you defer duty until goods actually sell, you avoid duty on anything you re-export, and you gain some options that are not available on a standard entry.

## The four benefits, and when each matters

- **Duty deferral.** You pay duty when goods leave the zone, not when they arrive, so you are not financing duty on inventory that is still sitting. The value is the cash-flow cost of that duty over the time goods are held, which rises with both the duty rate and the holding period.
- **Re-export elimination.** Goods re-exported from the zone avoid U.S. duty entirely. For an operation with meaningful re-exports, this is often the largest benefit.
- **Inverted tariff.** If a finished good made in the zone carries a lower duty rate than its imported components, you can pay the finished-good rate. This only helps where the rates actually invert, so it has to be checked per product.
- **MPF capping.** A zone can file a single weekly entry instead of one per shipment, which caps the [merchandise processing fee](/docs/glossary). For a high-volume importer, the saving is real.

## Bonded warehouse: deferral with less flexibility

A [bonded warehouse](/docs/glossary) also defers duty until goods are withdrawn, and goods withdrawn for export avoid duty. The trade-off is flexibility: handling is limited, manufacturing is generally not allowed, and storage is time-limited. For straightforward store-and-re-export flows it can be simpler than a zone; for manufacturing, high volume, or the inverted-tariff and weekly-entry benefits, a zone does more.

## The benefit is real but specific

None of this is free, and none of it is universal. A zone has setup and operating cost, and the benefits depend entirely on the flow: how much duty you defer and for how long, how much you re-export, whether your rates invert, and your shipment volume. The only honest way to decide is to model the benefit against your actual numbers, duty, holding periods, re-export share, and entry volume, and compare it to the cost. A generic "FTZs save money" is not a business case; a quantified one for your flow is.
