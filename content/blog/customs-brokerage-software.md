Most customs brokers do not lack tools. They have a classification lookup, a screening service, a spreadsheet for duty, and a separate interface for filing, and they rekey the same shipment into all of them. That is where errors and delays come from, not from any one tool being bad. Good customs brokerage software replaces that patchwork with one system that carries an entry from goods to filing without losing data along the way. Here is what it should actually do.

## Classification in the entry flow

Every entry starts with getting the [Harmonized Tariff Schedule](https://hts.usitc.gov/) code right, because the code drives duty, and a wrong code is both a cost and a liability. Software should help classify goods in the flow of building the entry, keep a history of how similar goods were classified before, and carry the duty rate straight into the calculation. Classification that lives in a separate lookup tool, disconnected from the entry, is where inconsistencies creep in.

## Screening on every party

US importers and brokers are required to screen the parties they deal with against [denied-party and sanctions lists](https://www.trade.gov/consolidated-screening-list). Doing this by hand does not scale, and a missed screen is the kind of gap that turns into a penalty. Capable software screens every counterparty automatically as the entry is built, flags matches for review, and records that the screen was run and what the result was. The record matters as much as the check.

## Duty and landed cost

Duty is only part of what an import actually costs. Freight, insurance, fees, and other charges all belong in landed cost, and getting that number right is what lets an importer price and account correctly. [Customs software](/services/customs-compliance) should calculate duty from the classification and roll it together with the other charges into a landed cost per line, rather than leaving that to a month-end spreadsheet.

## ACE-ready filing

US entries are filed electronically through CBP's [Automated Commercial Environment](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/automated). Software that is ACE-ready assembles the filing from the data you already captured during classification, screening, and documentation, then submits it. The alternative, re-entering everything into a separate ACE interface, is both slower and a fresh chance to introduce an error.

## Integration, because the data moves

A customs entry touches more than the broker. Data comes from client ERPs and arrives from carriers, and the filing goes to CBP. Software that cannot [exchange data](/services/trade-integrations) with those systems does not remove the rekeying; it just relocates it. When evaluating a platform, ask how it connects to CBP, to carriers over EDI, and to the systems your clients already run.

## An auditable record per entry

The quiet requirement behind all of this is proof. When CBP asks why a good was classified a certain way or whether a party was screened, the answer has to be on record, not reconstructed from memory. Every entry should carry a complete trail: the classification and its basis, the screening and its result, the duty and landed-cost calculation, the documents, and the filing. That record is what turns an audit from a scramble into a lookup.

## What it adds up to

The test of customs brokerage software is simple. Can a broker take an entry from goods to filed, with classification, screening, duty, documents, and the ACE submission in one flow, and show exactly what was done at each step? Software that can do that removes the rekeying, closes the compliance gaps, and speeds up clearance.
