A veterinary practice is a small healthcare business whose results come down to a few drivers: how many patients it sees, what each visit is worth, and how reliably patients come back. Most of the data to understand those drivers already sits in the practice management system, but that system is built to run the day-to-day, not to give owners clear reporting they can manage by. So practices often run on operational screens and gut feel rather than a clear view of their real performance. Power BI's role is to turn that data into governed reporting, so visit volume, average invoice, and patient recall are visible where the practice is actually managed. This is how veterinary practices use it.

## Running on operational screens, not management reporting

Ask a practice owner how the business is really doing, and the answer often comes from impressions and the practice management system's operational screens, rather than clear reporting of the trends that matter. The system holds visits, patients, services, and invoicing, and it runs the day-to-day well, but it rarely shows owners the **management view**: how visit volume and average invoice are trending, how the practice is performing over time, which patients are due back. So the owner manages by **feel**, with the data that would inform better decisions sitting unused in a system built for operations.

The first and most valuable thing Power BI does is build a **governed model** over that data and turn it into clear reporting of visit volume, average invoice, service mix, and recall over time. Power BI reads from the practice management system and financials, and the model organizes it into reporting the practice can actually manage by. Once that exists, the owner sees the practice's real drivers and trends, not just the operational screens. That shift, from running on operational data to managing on clear reporting, is the foundation, because the information to manage the practice well is already there; it just needs to be turned into a view that supports decisions.

## Visit volume and average invoice together

The practice's performance is best understood through **visit volume** and **average invoice** seen **together**, because each alone tells only half the story. **Volume** tells the practice how busy it is, how many patients it is seeing, but volume can rise while average invoice falls, or hold while it shifts, so busy does not always mean performing well. **Average invoice** tells the practice what each visit is **worth**, which, combined with volume, gives the real financial picture.

A governed model that shows **average invoice alongside visit volume** lets the practice see not just how many patients it sees but what each is worth and how that is trending. With both visible, the owner understands **performance**, not just **activity**: whether results are driven by seeing more patients, by higher-value visits, or by both, and where the trends are heading. This turns a vague busy-or-slow impression into a clear understanding of how the practice is actually doing financially, which is what the owner needs to manage it deliberately. For a business where the number of visits is only part of the story and the value of each matters just as much, seeing volume and value together is what reveals the real performance behind the daily activity.

## Patient recall: the recurring revenue to capture

Veterinary practices have a built-in advantage that is **easy to under-use**: patients return on **cycles**, check-ups, vaccinations, follow-ups, so there is recurring revenue in **recall**, bringing existing patients back when they are due. But without a clear view of **who is due**, recall opportunities slip, and the practice leaves reliable visits and revenue on the table, passively hoping owners remember to bring their pets in rather than actively reaching them.

A governed model that **surfaces patient recall**, identifying which patients are due for visits, vaccinations, or check-ups, lets the practice reach owners **deliberately.** This turns recall from a passive hope into an **active process**: the practice can see who is due and contact owners, bringing patients back rather than relying on them to return on their own. For a practice with a naturally **recurring patient base**, this is one of the most **reliable** ways to sustain visits and revenue, because the patients are already the practice's, and often the only thing standing between them and a return visit is a reminder. Seeing and acting on recall captures visits the practice has essentially already earned the right to, which makes it some of the highest-return activity it can do, and it depends entirely on being able to see who is due, which the governed model provides and the operational screens do not surface clearly.

## It fits practices of any size

A single-location practice benefits as clearly as a group, because even one practice generates visit, service, and financial data that owners read off operational screens rather than clear management reporting, usually while also practicing. Bringing it into a governed model gives a single practice clear reporting of its real drivers without a data team, through a partner who builds it and a managed arrangement that keeps it running. Groups gain the ability to compare locations, but the core value, seeing visit volume, average invoice, and recall clearly, applies to a single practice too, because the need to manage by the real drivers rather than operational screens is the same at any size.

## What veterinary practices end up with

A veterinary practice running on Power BI ends up with:

- Practice management and financial data turned into clear reporting of the real drivers.
- Visit volume and average invoice seen together, so performance is understood, not just activity.
- Patient recall surfaced, so recurring revenue from existing patients is actively captured.
- A management view of the practice instead of numbers read off operational screens.

The throughline is that a veterinary practice stops managing by feel and operational screens and starts managing on clear reporting of its real drivers, visit volume, average invoice, and recall, drawn from the data the practice management system already holds. Performance is understood through value as well as volume, the recurring revenue in recall gets actively captured, and the practice is run on evidence. For a small healthcare business whose results come down to visits, their value, and patients returning, turning the practice management data into clear reporting is what gives the owner the view needed to manage the practice deliberately rather than by impression.
