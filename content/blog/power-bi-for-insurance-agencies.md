An independent insurance agency is, in the end, its **book of business**: the portfolio of policies it has placed and the recurring commissions they generate. The agency's value and income depend on how that book is composed, how well it retains its clients, and how it is spread across carriers, and seeing all of that clearly is hard, because the data sits in the agency management system and commission records that run the day-to-day rather than giving owners a clear view of the book. Power BI's role is to turn that data into governed reporting, so the book, retention, and carrier mix are visible where the agency's value is actually built. This is how insurance agencies use it.

## Running on operational screens, not a view of the book

Ask an agency owner how the business is really doing, and the answer often comes from the agency management system's operational screens and a general sense of the book, rather than clear reporting of its composition, growth, and retention. The system holds policies, clients, and renewals, and commission data comes from carriers, but the **management view** of the **book**, how it is composed, how it is growing, how well it retains, how it is spread across carriers, is rarely clearly in one place. So the owner manages the agency's core asset by **feel**, with the data that would sharpen decisions sitting in systems built for operations.

The first and most valuable thing Power BI does is build a **governed model** over that data and turn it into clear reporting of the book of business, retention, commission, and carrier mix over time. Power BI reads from the agency management system and commission data, and the model organizes it into reporting the agency can manage by. Once that exists, the owner sees the book clearly, its composition and trends, rather than operational screens. That shift, from running on operational data to managing the book on clear reporting, is the foundation, because the agency's value is its book, and managing that value well requires seeing the book clearly, which the operational systems do not provide.

## The book of business: the agency's core asset

The **book of business** is the agency's **core asset** and the source of its value, so seeing its **composition and growth** is central to running the agency well. The book, the portfolio of policies and the commissions they generate, is what the agency is ultimately **worth**, and understanding it, which lines, which clients, which carriers, how it is growing, how concentrated it is, is understanding the business itself.

A governed model that shows the **book clearly** lets an agency see its composition and trends: how the book breaks down by line and client, where it is growing and shrinking, how concentrated it is in particular clients or lines. With that visible, the agency can **manage and grow the asset deliberately**, building the lines and segments that strengthen the book, watching concentration that creates risk, and understanding what is driving the book's growth. For a business whose **value is its book**, seeing the book clearly is directly seeing how to **build and protect** what the agency is worth, which is the core of what the reporting provides. An agency that can see its book manages its central asset on evidence; one that sees only operational screens manages it by impression, which for the thing that determines the agency's value is a real limitation.

## Retention: the backbone of recurring commission

Insurance commission is largely **recurring on renewals**, so the book is **sustained by retention**, which makes retention the **backbone** of the agency's economics. A client who **does not renew** is recurring commission **lost** that new business has to replace, so the book is only as stable as its retention, and because policies lapse **quietly** at renewal, attrition can erode the book without an obvious signal, one non-renewal at a time.

A governed model that **surfaces retention**, which policies and clients are renewing, which are at risk, which lines retain best, lets an agency **act to keep clients** rather than discovering attrition after the fact. The agency can see its retention rates and trends, identify clients and policies at risk ahead of renewal, and work to retain them, protecting the recurring commission the book generates. For an agency whose book depends on **renewals**, this is central, because retention is what keeps the book, and the recurring commission it produces, intact, and letting it erode undermines the asset. Seeing and managing retention is one of the highest-return things an agency can do, because the clients are already in the book and the commission is recurring, so keeping them is more efficient than replacing them, and the governed view is what makes the quiet erosion of non-renewals visible enough to manage.

## Carrier mix: commission and risk

An agency's book is spread across **carriers**, and the **carrier mix** shapes both **commission** and **risk**, so seeing it is part of managing the book well. Different carriers pay **different commissions** and carry different relationships and requirements, so how the book is **distributed** across carriers affects the agency's revenue and its **exposure** to any one carrier, concentration in a single carrier is a risk if that relationship changes.

A governed model that shows **carrier mix** lets an agency see how its book is spread, how dependent it is on each carrier, and how the mix affects commission. With that visible, the agency can manage its **carrier relationships and concentration** deliberately, balancing the book across carriers to manage both commission and risk, rather than letting the distribution drift. Seeing carrier mix is how an agency manages the carrier side of its book deliberately, which matters because the carrier relationships are central to the agency's economics and its risk, and a book that has drifted into heavy concentration with one carrier, or into lower-commission carriers, is exposed in ways only seeing the mix reveals. Managing carrier mix on evidence is part of managing the book as the valuable, and vulnerable, asset it is.

## It fits agencies of any size

A single agency benefits as clearly as a group, because even one agency runs policy, commission, and client data that owners read off operational screens rather than clear management reporting, usually while also producing and servicing. Bringing it into a governed model gives a single agency clear reporting of its book without a data team, through a partner who builds it and a managed arrangement that keeps it running. Groups gain the ability to compare offices, but the core value, seeing the book, retention, and carrier mix clearly, applies to a single agency too, because the agency's value is its book at any size.

## What insurance agencies end up with

An insurance agency running on Power BI ends up with:

- Agency management and commission data turned into clear reporting of the book.
- The book of business visible in composition and growth, so the core asset is managed deliberately.
- Retention surfaced, so the recurring commission the book generates is protected.
- Carrier mix visible, so commission and carrier concentration are managed.

The throughline is that an insurance agency stops managing its core asset by feel and operational screens and starts managing the book of business, retention, and carrier mix on clear reporting drawn from the data its systems already hold. The book, the thing the agency is worth, becomes visible and manageable, the retention that sustains it gets actively protected, and the carrier mix gets managed for commission and risk. For a business whose value is its book of recurring-commission policies, turning the agency and commission data into clear reporting is what gives the owner the view needed to build and protect the asset deliberately rather than by impression.
